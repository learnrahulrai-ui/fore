How an Indian promoter family actually uses the public market

The mechanism explained from scratch, then tested end to end against one company's filings — ASK Automotive Limited, FY21–FY26 audited. August 2026.

About seven in ten Indian listed companies are controlled by a family. That is not a footnote about governance — it changes what a listing is. The textbook version, written for markets where ownership is dispersed and managers are hired hands, says a company sells shares to raise money it needs, and the public becomes a part-owner alongside professional management. Almost none of that describes what happens when a family that already owns 100% of a profitable business decides to list it. The family does not need the money — if it did, it would not be profitable. It is not hiring anyone; it already runs the place and will keep running it. And it is not giving up control, because it is selling a slice small enough that it keeps three-quarters of the company afterwards.

So the honest question is: if a family that owns everything, needs no capital, and is surrendering no control decides to list — what did listing actually buy them? The answer is not obvious from the outside, and it is the whole subject of this page. It has nothing to do with growth capital and everything to do with a constraint that binds every rupee a family can pull out of a company it wholly owns, right up until the day it lists.

Here is the constraint. A promoter family with a private company has several ways to move money from the company to themselves, and every single one has a ceiling written into law or into a contract they have signed:

Route out of a private companyWho pays for itThe ceiling on it

DividendCompany cash, out of post-tax profitPro rata — every shareholder gets the same per share, so once outsiders exist you cannot pay only yourself

Selling sharesWhoever buys themNothing — but in a private company there is nobody to sell to

Read down that last column and the shape of the problem appears. Five of the six routes are capped, and they are capped in the same direction — all five drain the company's own bank account, which is exactly why the law, the auditor and the lender all have standing to object. The sixth route has no cap at all, because the money does not come from the company; it comes from a buyer. But in a private company that route is theoretical. There is no buyer. There is no price. A stake in an unlisted family firm is worth whatever a rare, negotiated, heavily-discounted private transaction says it is worth, and those happen once a decade if ever.

Which is what a listing actually does. It does not create a new way to take money out. It takes the one route that was never capped — selling shares — and supplies the missing ingredient: a permanent, liquid, daily-priced crowd of buyers, at a valuation set by the most optimistic of them rather than by a negotiation. After listing, the family can convert ownership into cash on any morning it chooses, in any size the market will absorb, without touching company cash, without asking a lender, without a statutory cap, and without the auditor having any say — because it is not a company transaction at all. The company is not a party to it. It appears in the company's accounts nowhere.

That last sentence is the one worth reading twice, because it is the reason this mechanism is nearly invisible to the ordinary tools people use to check on a company. A related-party rent shows up in the related-party note. A buyback shows up in the cash-flow statement. A salary shows up in the remuneration table. But a promoter selling ₹500 crore of stock to institutions appears in none of the financial statements, because no company money moved. It surfaces only in a shareholding-pattern filing and a bulk-deal record — two places that get read by nobody who is reading the annual report.

Before working through a real example, two more pieces of the machinery need naming, because without them the sums do not reach the size they reach.

The first is the bonus issue, and it is almost universally described wrongly, including in earlier versions of this page. A bonus issue hands every shareholder extra shares in proportion to what they already hold, free, by capitalising reserves the business genuinely earned. Because it is strictly pro rata, a bonus issue cannot change anybody's ownership percentage. Someone holding 40% before a 20-for-1 bonus holds 40% after. Anyone who tells you a bonus took a promoter from 2% to 50% has made an arithmetic error — that is not a thing a bonus can do.

What a bonus does do is two things that matter enormously here. It collapses the cost basis per share, because the same original cash is now spread across eighty-four times as many shares. And it manufactures share count — which is the thing that decides whether a company can be listed at all. A company with 42,353 shares in issue cannot have an IPO. There is no way to distribute 42,353 units among thousands of retail applicants and dozens of institutions, no way to strike a sensible per-share price, no way to satisfy minimum-lot and free-float requirements. Before any family firm can be sold to the public, its share count has to be inflated by orders of magnitude, and the bonus issue is how that is done, at zero cash cost, out of reserves. The bonus is not the extraction. It is the precondition for it — the step that turns an indivisible family holding into a divisible, sellable, market-priced pile.

The second piece is the multiple. A family that has spent thirty years building a business is, at listing, being paid not for the profit the company earned last year but for a multiple of it — thirty, forty, sometimes ninety times — and that multiple is the market's estimate of profits that have not happened yet. This is where the risk transfer sits, and it is worth being precise rather than indignant about it. A high multiple is not theft. Under ordinary finance arithmetic a company with no growth is fairly worth roughly the inverse of the required return, and anything above that is the price of a real, sustained growth expectation. What makes a sale into a high multiple lopsided is not the number. It is that the seller collects the entire present value of that future growth today, in cash, and retains none of the risk that it fails to arrive. The buyer inherits the obligation to be right, alone, having neither priced it nor timed it.

And there is one tell for whether a seller privately believes their own growth story: repetition. A single sale is genuinely ambiguous — diversification, estate planning, a house, a tax bill are all reasons a true believer might still sell some. A promoter who sells into every fresh wave of optimism is repeatedly expressing a private view that the price has outrun what they actually expect to deliver.

Which is the theory — so now test it against one company's filings

ASK Automotive Limited makes friction braking components and aluminium die-cast parts for two-wheelers. It is a real business: founded 1988, eighteen plants, roughly half the Indian market in two-wheeler brake shoes, customers including Hero, Honda, Bajaj, TVS, Yamaha and Suzuki, and an unmodified audit opinion from Walker Chandiok & Co LLP. Nothing below suggests otherwise, and that is exactly what makes it a useful test case — the mechanism described above does not require a fraud, and it is far easier to see when there isn't one.

Start with the cash the family ever put in, because everything downstream is measured against it. Between 1988 and 2005 there were thirteen cash subscriptions for equity, totalling ₹1.36 crore. The last of them is dated 23 May 2005. After that: nothing, for eighteen years. The business funded itself out of retained profit, which is the genuinely creditable part of this story and should not be waved past. Then the share count was built:

Two bonus issues multiplied the count 84-fold, and that is what made a listing mechanically possible: 42,353 shares could never have been offered to the public; 197 million could. The same arithmetic collapses the cost basis. The RHP carries a certificate from B.B. & Associates, dated 12 June 2023, putting the promoters' weighted-average cost of acquisition at ₹0.02 per share. That figure is not a scandal to be explained — it is ₹1.36 crore of genuine 1988–2005 cash divided across a share count inflated 84 times by capitalising reserves the business really did earn. It is arithmetic. But it means the shares were then sold at an issue price of ₹282, and the gap between ₹0.02 and ₹282 is the entire economic content of the transaction.

Now watch the two channels run side by side. Before listing, the family used the capped routes, and used them hard — two buybacks in consecutive years consumed 89% and 90% of those years' profits, and every rupee of it came out of company cash under a lender's covenants and a 23% tax. These two sub-totals must be read separately and never merged into a single sentence. The ₹323.39 crore left the company's own bank account and reduced what was available to every shareholder. The ₹1,745.61 crore never touched the company at all — it moved from buyers to sellers, and ASK Automotive was not a party to it. Different payer, different tax treatment, different meaning. Adding them produces a true total of what the family received, but the composition is the finding, not the sum.

Set the two sub-totals against time and the step-change is the whole argument. Across FY21–FY23, under every cap the law and the lenders imposed, the family drew roughly ₹72 crore a year, all of it company cash. Across the twenty-five months from November 2023, through three share sales, they drew roughly ₹856 crore a year, none of it company cash. That is an 11.8-fold change in rate, and the payer changed from the company to the public market. Nothing about the business changed on listing day. What changed was who was standing on the other side of the transaction.

Debt grew 66.2% while profit grew 20.1% — more than three times as fast. Operating cash flow of ₹301.19 crore did not cover capital expenditure of ₹483.64 crore, leaving a hole of ₹182.45 crore that was filled with ₹260.59 crore of net new borrowing. And in that same year the dividend per share was raised 23.3%. A company can do all of this legitimately — it is building capacity, and capacity takes cash before it produces any. But it means the money reaching the family in FY26 was not surplus the business threw off. It came from lenders and from the market.

Which raises the question of what the new capital is earning, and here the honest answer needs both halves stated. The company reports a headline ROACE of 26.9%, and every broker note leads with it — but that number is computed across plants built decades ago and long since depreciated, mixed with plants half-built and half-empty. Taking FY26's incremental profit of ₹49.70 crore against its incremental capital expenditure of ₹483.64 crore gives 10.3%, against a cost of debt of around 8.8%. That is a real arithmetic comparison and it is a fair prompt for concern — but it is not a clean return-on-capital measure and should not be presented as one. A single year's capex does not operate for a full year, the incremental profit reflects price, volume, mix, interest and tax as well as the new assets, and the Karoli plant that absorbed the bulk of the spend has been sitting at 65% utilisation for a year against a 1.75x asset-turnover target. The defensible statement is that new capital has not yet demonstrated it earns materially more than it costs to borrow, and that the headline 26.9% describes the old business rather than the new.

So who is on the other side, and did they get paid

Every rupee of that ₹1,745.61 crore came from a buyer, and what happened to those buyers is the part no earnings call revisits. Two sales, thirteen months apart, tested it directly.

In September 2024 the promoter sold 6.05% through an open-market offer for sale at a floor of ₹433, into a stock that had just closed at an all-time-high ₹469.25. Within ten sessions the price was ₹388.95 — down 17.1%. In November 2025 he sold 4.00% as a single pre-arranged block: 7,885,704 shares, matched almost share-for-share by SBI Mutual Fund taking 7,861,739 at ₹473 for ₹371.86 crore. The price moved −0.01%. Same seller, comparable size, thirteen months apart, and the only material difference was whether the shares went to a crowd or to one institution that had already agreed the price. On the same quantity, the avoided price impact is worth roughly ₹58 crore — an estimate, not a filed figure, but the 17.1% against 0.01% is filed and is not in dispute.

Then look at what those buyers did afterwards, because that is the closest thing to a verdict the record contains. Allspring Emerging Markets Equity Fund, which took 2,559,155 shares in the quarter of the first sale, has held exactly 2,559,155 shares — the identical count — for seven consecutive quarters. Not one share traded in two years. The SBI schemes that absorbed the second block sat frozen at 7,908,563 shares for three quarters. Over the very same window, holders who had not absorbed a promoter sale kept adding: Goldman Sachs went from 2,931,145 shares to 4,593,382, SBI Life from 2,594,587 to 6,036,170, quarter after quarter. Same stock, same period, opposite behaviour. A position frozen at precisely the count it absorbed is not a view about the company. It is a view about the price that particular buyer paid.

Both sales also landed in quarters that turned out, only with hindsight, to be local earnings peaks — the September 2024 quarter printed EPS of ₹3.41, a record not exceeded for four quarters; the December 2025 quarter printed ₹4.05, tying the all-time high, after which the next quarter fell 10.4% and has not recovered it. Timing that lands twice at a high-water mark cannot prove intent, and this page does not assert any. It is simply the pattern the filings contain.

What the mechanism actually is. Not fraud, and not a rich multiple — the multiple here compressed while ₹2,069 crore reached the family. It is this: a genuine business is built over thirty years on ₹1.36 crore of real cash; reserves it truly earned are capitalised into an 84-fold share count that makes it listable at all; the listing supplies the one thing a private company lacks, which is a permanent crowd of buyers; and thereafter ownership converts to cash on any morning the family chooses, at a price set by the most optimistic buyer available, with no lender's consent, no statutory cap, no auditor's signature and no line in any financial statement. The public's money bought a share of future growth. The family's shares bought the present value of that same growth, in cash, today — and kept none of the risk that it arrives. Every step was disclosed. No step was ever connected to the next in any single document, which is the only reason it is not obvious.

And that is why the historical comparison is worth making carefully rather than dismissing. A feudal landholder's income came from rent on land granted rather than bought, paid by tenants who carried the entire risk of a bad harvest. The resemblance is not in any of the specifics — it is in the separation of who bears the production risk from who collects the surplus, and in the way both arrangements pass intact to the next generation. It is a parallel about structure, not an accusation about conduct, and it is offered as an interpretation of the numbers above rather than as an additional fact.

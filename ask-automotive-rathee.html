Verdict. This is a real business with real products, real customers and real revenue growth. But its founding family has taken ₹2,069 crore out since 2021 — through two buybacks that consumed 89% and 90% of those years' profits, an IPO that sent the company nothing, and two secondary sales — while borrowings rose 8.5x and free cash flow turned negative. FY26's entire margin improvement came from losing a customer account, not from operations. New capital is earning 10.3% against an 8.8% cost of debt, behind a headline ROACE of 26.9%. And every window that opens on one of the promoter's own sale dates has lost to gold. The one window the public won is the one before he started selling.

What was built, and what was taken

Kuldip Singh Rathee is a first-generation industrialist. He began manufacturing two-wheeler brake shoes at a single plant in Gurugram in 1989, and over thirty-odd years built that into eighteen plants and roughly half the Indian market for two-wheeler friction braking components. The customer list is the entire Indian two-wheeler industry — Hero for twenty-nine years, Honda for twenty-two, Bajaj twenty-one, Yamaha twenty, TVS nineteen, Suzuki fifteen. None of what follows should be read as suggesting the business is fake. It is not. That is precisely what makes the rest of it worth understanding.

The company sells four things. Aluminium lightweighting precision solutions — high-pressure die-cast crank cases, engine covers, housings, now alloy wheels — is the largest at 50.8% of FY26 revenue. Advanced Braking Systems is 36.9%. Safety control cables are 3.8%, and the low-margin wheel assembly business, 5.0%, was wound to zero on 1 April 2026. Aluminium overtook braking during FY26; it was 31.7% of revenue in FY21.

That segment name deserves attention, because it is doing work. "Advanced Braking Systems" is not ABS. The company's own product page lists the segment's contents: brake shoes, brake panel assemblies, brake pads, clutch plates, brake linings, clutch shoes, disc brake pads, brake liners. A brake shoe is a drum-brake friction part — the annual report says so directly: "Brake shoes are friction components used in drum brake systems." There is no electronic control unit, no hydraulic modulator, no wheel-speed sensor. The strongest single fact here: the word "anti-lock" appears zero times in the 462-page prospectus and zero times in the 344-page FY26 annual report.

The company's formal glossary is careful about this — it defines the abbreviation as "AB — Advanced Braking", dropping the S. The marketing layer is not careful. The Q4 FY26 investor deck labels its own revenue pie chart "ABS, 36.9%", and annual report page 15 is headed "Advanced Braking Systems (ABS)." The collision is manufactured in house, and it is already working: on one earnings call, an analyst asked about growth in "the ABS segment" meaning friction parts, in the same session where two other analysts used "ABS" to mean the anti-lock mandate.

The distinction is not academic, because India has proposed mandating genuine anti-lock ABS on every two-wheeler. Compliance means replacing a front drum with a disc plus an anti-lock module — which deletes the front brake panel assembly, the highest-value item ASK makes in that segment. ASK does not manufacture anti-lock units. Bosch, Continental, Nissin, ZF, Brakes India and Endurance Technologies do. Endurance also competes with ASK in friction — so the competitor is hedged against the mandate and ASK is not.

Which brings us to why the company went public, and the answer is not the one the word "IPO" normally implies. The company received nothing. The prospectus says so three separate times, and the capital table proves it: shares outstanding were 197,142,600 before the Offer and 197,142,600 after. Not one new share was created. The objects clause, verbatim:

"The objects of the Offer are to (i) carry out the Offer for Sale of up to 29,571,390 Equity Shares by the Promoter Selling Shareholders... Our Company will not receive any proceeds from the Offer and the Offer Proceeds will be received by the Promoter Selling Shareholders."

Two people sold, and it is worth being precise about who they are, because the names alone conceal it. Kuldip Singh Rathee, Chairman and Managing Director, took ₹583.74 crore. Mrs. Vijay Rathee — his wife, the mother of Joint Managing Directors Prashant and Aman Rathee, and herself a Non-Executive Director on the board — took ₹250.17 crore. The company's own Regulation 30 disclosure of May 2026 states the relationship plainly: "Spouse of Mr. Kuldip Singh Rathee, Chairman & Managing Director. Mother of Mr. Prashant Rathee and Mr. Aman Rathee, Joint Managing Directors." There was no private equity investor, no venture fund, no financial backer being repaid — the company was 100% family-owned before listing. Nobody was exiting; a husband and wife were converting paper into money. Their certified cost of acquisition, signed off by B.B. & Associates on 12 June 2023, was ₹0.02 per share against an issue price of ₹282. The sons, Prashant and Aman, sold nothing.

So if the company needed no money, what did the IPO actually solve? The prospectus answers that too, in the sections nobody reads — and the pattern starts five years earlier than the listing. Consider thirty-three days in the spring of 2018, each event separately disclosed, none of them ever mentioned on an earnings call:

Read in order: the company created a nine-year rental annuity on its own factory, sold the factory to the Chairman's wife a month later, and handed her the income stream — payable by the company's own joint venture. Five days after that, a family-connected company merged in; A.A. Autotech's director until the merger was Prashant Rathee. Sitting on the balance sheet from that period is ₹181.91 crore of goodwill — identical in FY21, FY22, FY23 and again in FY26. Six years, never impaired, never written down, never once discussed on any of the eight earnings calls. It is 13.9% of net worth. Three things could not be verified from the documents obtained: the price ASK-9 was sold at, the consideration paid for A.A. Autotech, and an explicit note tying that goodwill to that scheme — the dates and the frozen balance are consistent with it, but the confirming note sits in an FY19 or FY20 annual report not published on the exchanges.

Nine-tenths of profit, two years running, paid out to the shareholders — who were, in their entirety, the family. And while that cash left, the company borrowed to fund its factories: total borrowings went ₹79.9 crore (FY21) to ₹159.8 crore (FY22) to ₹318.0 crore (FY23). Meanwhile margins were collapsing under aluminium, which the prospectus discloses moved from ₹128/kg to ₹181 to ₹197 — material cost rose from 65.77% to 70.39% of revenue, and EBITDA margin fell from 13.40% to 9.00%. FY22 revenue grew 30% while profit fell 22%.

So the correlation is this. The family had found a way to convert company cash into personal cash — the buyback — but a buyback is capped by available cash, taxed at roughly 23%, and, per risk factor 25, required lender consent under the company's own loan covenants. Every one of those constraints was binding precisely when margins were weakest and debt was rising fastest. The IPO removed all three at once. It converted an illiquid family holding into a marketable security at 45.6x earnings, and it created a permanent mechanism — the secondary sale — for extracting cash that never again has to touch the company's balance sheet or ask a lender's permission.

The prospectus disclosed a great deal honestly: 60.83% of revenue from three customers, 90.65% from a single sector, adverse CARO matters in two years, a related-party book that hit 21.10% of total income in FY22, and an Enforcement Directorate order under FEMA dated 23 May 2023 — one month before filing — demanding every import and export record to date. What it did not contain was any risk factor about braking regulation. Anti-lock mandates appear in the document only inside the industry chapter the company commissioned and paid CRISIL to write, where they are framed as a tailwind for content per vehicle.

There is one more thing the prospectus disclosed, and it is the single most important passage in this entire file. Under the heading "Conversion of wheel assembly to job-work," it sets out the economics of what was then 13.75% of revenue:

A 1.90% gross spread — before labour, before overhead, before depreciation. This was not a business; it was pass-through billing of a customer's own components, and it accounted for one rupee in every seven of the revenue the IPO was priced on. Worse, it was the fastest-growing major line in the document: up 42.79% in FY22 against company growth of 30.65%, and 45.87% in FY23 against 26.90% — expanding at 1.4 and 1.7 times the company rate in precisely the two years shown to buyers at 45.6 times earnings.

And then the prospectus says what it intends to do about it, in June 2023, five months before the offer:

"We propose to convert our wheel assembly arrangements into job-work agreements in the coming years. While this will lead to a reduction in our overall revenue from operations, there is no significant impact on profitability expected... Furthermore, as a result of this conversion, certain metrics such as our PAT Margin are likely to show an improvement due to the lower profitability margins in our wheel assembly business category."
Read that against what happened next. The prospectus states plainly, in advance, that removing this revenue would make the margin look better for no operational reason whatsoever — it is disclosed as an accounting consequence, and explicitly as having "no significant impact on profitability." Two years later, in May 2025, the identical event was presented to the market as an achievement: "we will be improving by 150 basis points... 80 basis points will come because the wheel assembly has gone." By December 2025 the sell-side had turned it into "structural margin expansion underway" and a ₹593 target. The margin gain was pre-announced in the offer document as arithmetic. It was re-sold two years later as performance.

The second half of that sentence did not survive either. The prospectus promised a conversion to job-work — keep doing the work, bill a fee instead of the gross amount, lose the revenue but keep the profit. What actually happened was the loss of the account: "the customer has shifted 60% business," "once the customer takes it back," and revenue nil from 1 April 2026. A billing change was disclosed; a customer departure occurred. Because the spread was under 2%, almost no profit went with it — which is exactly why the arithmetic works so flatteringly, and exactly why calling it a strategic upgrade is generous.

Eight months after listing, the plan for the shares themselves was stated out loud. Asked on the July 2024 call about the 85% family holding, Joint MD Aman Rathee answered without hesitation: "we have diluted 15% and for the next 10% as per the SEBI guidelines, we still have around 2 years to dilute it... we will surely inform you as and when we descend." That is exactly what happened — 6.05% in September 2024, 4.00% in November 2025, totalling 10.05% against the 10% named, delivered in sixteen months rather than twenty-four.

But the group-level number conceals what actually moved. Cross the prospectus's certified individual holdings against the shareholding pattern filed on the listing date itself, and the two do not agree — because between the prospectus and the listing, the two sons transferred 32,658,023 shares — 16.57% of the company — to their parents, who then sold ₹833.91 crore in the IPO. Set out plainly, the board and the register are the same four people. Kuldip Singh Rathee is Chairman and Managing Director. His wife, Mrs. Vijay Rathee, is a Non-Executive Director and the company's largest shareholder. Their two sons, Prashant and Aman, are both Joint Managing Directors. Between them the four hold 74.95% of a ₹10,162 crore listed company, and the two who run it day-to-day are the two whose stakes were reduced to 4.90% to fund their parents' ₹833.91 crore. Four independent directors — Deepti Sehgal, Kumaresh Chandra Misra, Vinay Kumar Piparsania and Yogesh Kapur — were all re-appointed for second three-year terms in a single board resolution in January 2026; each declared no relationship with any existing director, and nothing in the filings contradicts that.

Two further things follow from the same filings. First, Mrs. Vijay Rathee has never sold a single share since: 68,934,794 shares from the listing date through 30 June 2026, unchanged through both of her husband's later sales. At 34.97% she is the largest shareholder in the company — a bigger holder than the Chairman himself, whose stake is now 30.18%. She sits on the board as a Non-Executive Director, was re-appointed by rotation at the board meeting of 19 May 2026 subject to shareholder approval at the 38th AGM, and holds a master's degree in zoology after seventeen years as an officer of Punjab & Sind Bank. She does not appear on the management panel of any of the eight earnings calls, and is not mentioned once on any of them. Second, Kuldip's two later sales took him from 40.23% to 30.18%, so across the entire episode — a ₹1,495 crore personal cash-out over three transactions — his stake fell 11.15 points while his son's fell 9.40.

Three things only become visible once the years sit side by side. First, the rent escalates every single year — each son's rent rises ₹2.38 crore to ₹3.03 crore, up 27% over six years, on property let to the company they run. Second, directors' sitting fees and commission did not exist before FY24: the FY23 comparative column in the annual report is blank across every name. That payment channel opened in the first full year after listing. And third, within it, Mrs. Vijay Rathee — a promoter and the largest shareholder — draws the same ₹0.30 crore commission as each genuinely independent director.

One more thing about that CSR line, which matters more than its size. ASK's annual report lists every CSR project it funds — vocational training in Jhajjar, education in Gurgaon, Rewa, Ghaziabad, Prayagraj and Delhi, rural sports in Faridabad, Sonipat and Bhiwani, environmental work in Gurgaon. Across the whole document there is exactly one CSR registration number: CSR00000244, AHSAAS. Not most of the programme — all of it. Every rupee of a statutory obligation under Section 135 flows through a single implementing agency that the company's own related-party note classifies as an entity "in which directors of the Company and their relatives are able to exercise significant influence."

The buyback line deserves a second look, because the schedule names one person: "Buyback of Securities — Equity Shares: Kuldip Singh Rathee." Not the family, not the promoter group. The entire ₹149.93 crore — nine-tenths of two years' profit — went to the Chairman individually.

Two smaller lines say as much about the structure as the large one. The two sons each collected ₹2.62 crore of rent from the company in FY23 against ₹2.34 crore of salary — each of them earned more from renting property to the company than from running it. And the company's mandatory CSR spending, ₹5.62 crore over three years, was routed to Ahsaas Trust, itself classified in the schedule as an entity the directors and their relatives can significantly influence.

Separately from those transfers there is trade. AA Friction Materials, another entity under directors' influence, took ₹305.14 crore of goods and job-work from the company across the six years — and the mix inverted: purchases of goods fell ₹45.11 crore (FY23) to ₹17.36 crore (FY26) while job-work services rose ₹8.25 crore to ₹21.14 crore over the same span. Real goods came the other way, so this is not extraction in the sense the table above is; it is disclosed related-party trade. But brake liners are the core input of the braking segment, and the margin on them accrues outside the listed company.

And there is risk taken on behalf of the family's other vehicles. Across the six years the company extended ₹1,021 crore of corporate guarantees and ₹479.75 crore of loans to ASK Automobiles Private Limited — a subsidiary the prospectus discloses as having negative net worth — with both accelerating after listing (₹330 crore of guarantees and ₹219.40 crore of fresh loans in FY26 alone). To be fair to the company, ASK Automobiles is wholly owned, so this is intra-group funding that eliminates on consolidation and is not money leaving for the family. It is, though, exposure: a guarantee appears in no cash-flow statement, and it is public shareholders who now stand behind it.

Which brings back the ASK-9 lease, and why it matters more than its size. Rent paid by the company to related parties is disclosed — the sons' ₹2.62 crore each is right there in the schedule. But the ASK-9 rent is not in the schedule at all, and cannot be, because after March 2018 the company is not a party to it. The tenant is ASK Fras-Le Friction, the company's own 49% joint venture; the landlord is the Chairman's wife. The company built the plant, signed a nine-year lease over it, then sold the asset out — leaving a rental stream that runs from a company-controlled entity to a promoter, through a contract the listed company originated, disclosed nowhere in its own related-party table. The prospectus records the three deeds. It does not record the rent.

The change the IPO made was not to the routes. It was to who pays. Before listing, every channel was capped: a buyback is limited by the cash in the company, taxed at roughly 23%, and — per risk factor 25 — required lender consent; remuneration is capped by the Companies Act; rent must be arm's-length and audit-committee approved; dividends must go to everyone pro rata. Under all of those constraints the family extracted ₹216.90 crore over three years — about ₹72 crore a year, every rupee of it out of company cash. After listing, three share sales moved ₹1,745.61 crore in twenty-five months — about ₹856 crore a year, with no cash leaving the company at all, no lender asked, no tax on buyback, no statutory cap. An 11.8-fold step-change in rate, and the payer switched from the company to the public market. That is the whole function of the listing, and it is visible only when the pre-IPO schedule and the post-IPO filings are laid side by side.

Two readings come out of that trace, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is a genuine defence of the family. Only ₹1.36 crore of cash was ever subscribed for equity in this company, the last rupee of it in 2005, and everything since was built out of retained profit. Bonus issues do not manufacture value; they capitalise reserves the business actually earned. Thirty-five years of compounding a brake-shoe business into an eighteen-plant group is not financial engineering, and no amount of forensic reading changes that.

The second is the timing. 98.9% of the shares that existed on IPO day were created free, by two bonus issues — 20-for-1 in March 2018 and 3-for-1 in March 2021 — and only 1.07% of the count was ever subscribed for cash. That is the entire explanation of the ₹0.02 certified cost basis; it is not a mystery, it is arithmetic. And both bonus issues sit directly against extraction events: the 2018 one falls the day after the ASK-9 plant was sold to the Chairman's wife and four days before the amalgamation, and the 2021 one falls five months before the first buyback. The share count needed for a ₹833.91 crore offer was assembled first, at nil cost, and drawn on afterwards.

The trace also settles where the sons' stakes came from, which matters because those stakes funded the IPO. About 90% of Prashant Rathee's 28,188,000 shares originated in the A.A. Autotech amalgamation — 6,354,000 issued to him for non-cash consideration in July 2019, multiplied to 25,416,000 by the 2021 bonus. Four years later he transferred 18,528,012 of them to his father, who sold ₹583.74 crore in the offer. So the pipeline runs: family-connected company merges in at a ₹181.91 crore goodwill cost to public-company-to-be → shares issued to a son for no cash → tripled by a free bonus → passed to the father → sold to the public. Every step disclosed. No step connected to the next in any document.

Over the same period company borrowings went from ₹79.9 crore to ₹679 crore — up 8.5 times. In FY26 the audited consolidated accounts show operations generating ₹301.19 crore, capital expenditure consuming ₹483.64 crore, and the ₹182.45 crore hole filled with ₹260.59 crore of net new borrowing, against reported profit of ₹297.32 crore. The money the family extracted did not come from the business generating surplus cash. It came from the capital markets and from lenders.

And the capital being raised is not earning what the headline returns suggest. The company reports ROACE of 26.9%, and every broker note leads with it. But that figure describes the legacy Gurugram business, built decades ago and long since depreciated. The relevant question for anyone buying the stock today is what the new money earns — and FY26 answers it precisely, because that was the year of peak capital deployment:

The pattern is not that he misses everything — the plants, the growth rate, the wheel-assembly exit and the returns all landed. It is which promises land. Operational commitments to shareholders slip, shrink or get quietly restated. The one commitment delivered ahead of schedule, in full, was the one that moved ₹911.7 crore into his own account.

The margin story deserves its own look, because it is the load-bearing beam of every bull case written on this company. Removing a low-margin business raises the average margin without a single rupee of operational improvement — the arithmetic does it for you. Management guided to a 150 basis point improvement built from two halves: 80 points from the wheel-assembly exit, and 70 points from "internal more efficiencies and economies of scale." The margin moved exactly 80 points. The operational half delivered nothing at all. A December 2025 broker note nonetheless headlined "structural margin expansion underway, driven by mix, scale and portfolio rationalization" and set a ₹593 target, six days after the promoter sold ₹372 crore, on a page whose shareholding chart still read "Promoters 79%" when the true figure was already 74.95%.

And the exit was not a decision. It was a loss, described in the Chairman's own words on two separate calls: "we were requesting the customer for the last two years to shift this business to someone else... Now the customer has shifted 60% business from Q4 FY25" (May 2025), and "the customer has assured that by March end the balance will also go, which is about 48%... let us see once the customer takes it back" (January 2026). The customer removed the business. It was reported to the market as "strategic reduction" and "portfolio rationalization," and the resulting arithmetic was sold as structural margin expansion.

In FY22 and FY23 wheel assembly was the fastest-growing line in the mix, nearly doubling from ₹168 crore to ₹353 crore, and reported growth ran above the ex-wheel figure in both years. Those are precisely the two years shown in the prospectus — the growth record the IPO was priced on at 45.6 times earnings. Nobody stripped it out then. The moment the line turned down, it became "strategically reduced," and every growth number since has been quoted without it. The segment was worth including while it flattered the multiple and worth excluding once it did not.

Which closes the loop with the prospectus. That document already told buyers this revenue carried a 1.90% gross spread and that removing it would lift the PAT margin for no operational reason. So the sequence, end to end, is: a near-zero-margin pass-through inflates the growth rate the offer is priced on; the offer document simultaneously discloses that the same line will be removed and will flatter margins when it goes; the promoters sell ₹833.91 crore; two years later the pre-announced arithmetic arrives on schedule and is presented as a margin achievement; the sell-side capitalises it into a target price; and the promoter sells another ₹372 crore six days before that note is published. Every individual step was disclosed. The sequence was never assembled anywhere — which is the only reason it works.

The accounts are arranged the same way. Note 5 of every quarterly filing says:

"The Group is primarily engaged in the manufacturing of auto components including advanced braking systems, aluminium lightweighting precision solutions and safety control cables... Hence, there are no other reportable segments in terms of requirements of Ind AS 108 'Operating Segments'."

The company declares itself a single segment. Yet every investor deck splits revenue four ways and every call quotes segment growth rates. So the segment numbers exist and management reviews them — but there is no audited segment revenue, no segment profit, nothing. The one figure needed to size the anti-lock mandate against this company is the braking segment's revenue and margin. He will not give it on a call. The accounts are built so you cannot compute it. The auditor signed clean, so this sits within the letter of the standard; but a company telling the market it runs four businesses and telling its auditor it runs one has ensured its largest disclosed risk cannot be independently measured.

And that risk does not land where the name suggests. The one time it was quantified, the Chairman placed it precisely: "the total headwinds are about Rs. 230 crore... they are into the aluminium segment." That is consistent with the engineering — the front brake panel is an aluminium high-pressure die-cast part, so an anti-lock mandate deletes an aluminium component, not a friction one. Which means the disclosed regulatory exposure sits on ALPS — the 50.8%-of-revenue segment that every bull case, including the broker note above, presents as the growth engine and the source of the margin expansion. There is no audited ALPS revenue or margin either.

Three smaller things from the same filings. The EBITDA margin quoted as 13.1% is struck on total income and includes ₹20.03 crore of other income — on revenue alone the operating margin is 12.7%, and other income grew 68.6% against revenue's 16.0%. Trade receivables rose 55.1% against 16.0% revenue growth, with other current assets up 114.9%; the CFO attributed this to a March aluminium spike, which on the arithmetic explains roughly a quarter of it. And goodwill of ₹181.91 crore — 13.9% of net worth — sits unchanged on the balance sheet, unmentioned on any of the eight calls.

The first time, he sold at the all-time high into the open market and blew a 17% hole in the price within ten sessions. The second time, one buyer took the entire block — his 7,885,704 shares were 87% of that day's total volume — and the price did not move at all. On the same quantity, avoiding the first sale's price impact was worth roughly ₹58 crore.

The quarterly filings name who absorbed each one. Three institutions appear for the first time in the September 2024 quarter, the quarter of the first sale — Allspring Emerging Markets Equity Fund (2,559,155 shares), Abu Dhabi Investment Authority–Monsoon (2,426,449) and Florida Retirement System–Allspring (2,092,345). Together that is 7,077,949 shares, 59% of what was sold, and Florida Retirement System had also been an anchor investor in the IPO itself. What happened next is the part worth sitting with: Allspring Emerging Markets has held 2,559,155 shares — identical to the share — for seven consecutive quarters, September 2024 through June 2026. Two full years without a single share traded. The SBI schemes that took the November 2025 block did the same thing, frozen at 7,908,563 through April, May and June 2026. Over the identical period Goldman Sachs went 2,931,145 to 4,593,382 and SBI Life 2,594,587 to 6,036,170, adding in every single quarter. Same stock, same window: one set of holders accumulating continuously, another set motionless from the moment they absorbed a promoter sale.

Both sales also landed in the quarter that marked a local earnings peak. The September 2024 quarter printed EPS of ₹3.41, a record not exceeded for four quarters. The December 2025 quarter printed ₹4.05, tying the all-time high — the next quarter fell 10.4% to ₹3.63, and it has not been beaten since.

The public still lost to gold, because of what it was charged at the door and what has been sold into it since.

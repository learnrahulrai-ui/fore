Promoter Reports

Forensic equity research on Indian listed companies — written for the people whose money is in them.

About seventy per cent of India's listed companies are controlled by families. That single fact should decide how they are analysed, and mostly it does not. Research written here largely follows a template built elsewhere, for markets where ownership is dispersed and managers are hired agents. It rates the business and treats the owner as background.

In India the owner is the variable. A promoter family decides what the company buys and from whom, what it pays in rent and to whom, which of its plants it sells and to which relative, when it lists, how much it sells into that listing, and what it says on the call afterwards. None of that is a footnote to the investment case. It is the investment case. So these reports start from the promoter and work outward, and they are built entirely from primary documents — prospectuses, exchange filings, XBRL shareholding data, insider disclosures, audited accounts and earnings-call transcripts — never from a summary of any of those.

Every report answers one question before any other: did money left in this company beat gold? Not whether the multiple looks fair or the growth reads respectably. Indian households hold gold as the real alternative, so gold is the benchmark, and real inflation of ten per cent a year is the floor. A stock that clears neither has cost its holder money, however the earnings looked.

How these are written

The sources, the tests and the standards — including what these reports refuse to do — are set out on the standards page. In short: nothing here is forecast, nothing is rated, and every figure traces to a document you can open yourself. Where something could not be verified, the report says so in the same sentence rather than in a footnote. Where an earlier version was wrong, the correction stays visible on the page instead of quietly disappearing.

Who paid, who received, and what did a receiver do with it?

Tata Technologies Limited, slips first sold to a public in November 2023. Compiled 3 November 2026, and corrected that same day — see a note at a foot of this page.

A word first, because every sentence after it depends on a word meaning one thing and not another.

A company is divided into slips. This one is divided into 40.5 crore of them. Hold a slip and you hold one part in 40.5 crore of whatever it earns, for as long as you hold. Three ways of counting agree on that number, which is worth showing since much on this page rests upon it.

How many slips exist, counted three ways: (1)  share capital ₹81 crore ÷ face value ₹2  =  40.5 crore
(2)  market value ₹26,312 crore ÷ price ₹648  =  40.60 crore
(3)  4,62,75,000 slips sold, stated as 11.41 per cent  →  ÷ 0.1141  =  40.56 crore

Three roads, one answer. From here, one part in a hundred of this company is about 40.5 lakh slips.

Now a sale, told as a transfer and not as an event, because a difference between those two is this whole page.

Who paid, and whose hand did that money reach?

Between 22 and 24 November 2023, slips were sold at ₹500 each, coming to ₹3,042.5 crore. Demand ran to 69.43 times what was on offer, and men who manage institutions bid 203 times their portion.

Here is a part that gets reported as "a company raised ₹3,042 crore", and that sentence is wrong in a way worth dwelling on. No new slips were cut. Slips already held changed hands. So money went from thousands of buyers into three holders' hands:

Put it on a field and it stops being abstract. A man owns a hundred bighas. He sells fifteen of them to villagers who pool their money. Fifteen bighas now belong to those villagers, and his pocket holds their money. No well was dug. No tractor arrived. No bund was repaired. A farm stands as it was that morning, and a man who owned it is richer by what villagers paid.

That is not wrongdoing, and nothing here suggests it is. An owner may sell what he owns. A point is only that a sentence "a company raised money" describes a different thing — money entering a company's own account, to do something nobody has yet told us — and no such thing happened here.

Six weeks earlier, a same parent had sold to a different buyer, and that price belongs beside ₹500.

October 2023: 9.9 per cent sold to buyers led by TPG for ₹1,613.7 crore. What did one slip cost them? (1)  9.9 per cent of 40.5 crore slips  =  4.01 crore slips
(2)  ₹1,613.7 crore ÷ 4.01 crore slips  =  ₹402 a slip
(3)  ₹402 in October, ₹500 in November  =  a step up of 24.4 per cent in six weeks

Step (1) assumes 40.5 crore slips existed in October as in November. If slips were cut between those dates, every figure here moves.

On 30 November 2023 those slips became buyable by anyone. Trading opened at ₹1,200 — 140 per cent above a price allotted buyers had paid — and touched ₹1,400 that day. Which gives a question worth asking door by door instead of once: what does a buyer at each door hold today, with a slip at ₹648?

Read a profit row across. A best harvest this company has had was FY24, a first full year after its slips became buyable. FY25 was flat. FY26 fell by near a fifth, to below FY23 — a last full year a buyer saw before paying. Money coming in grew every year throughout. What grew did not reach profit.

And whatever was shown to a buyer in 2023 covered FY21 to FY23: growth of 48 then 25 per cent in money coming in, 83 then 43 per cent in profit. Those were a fastest years this company has had. Nothing since has come near them.

Which raises a question a farmer would ask first and a market seems not to have asked at all: who was buying this work, and was anyone worried about them?

Offer papers named five main buyers of this company's work. Two were a parent and a parent's British arm. A third was VinFast, a Vietnamese maker of electric cars, a customer since 2018 and described in those papers as a significant part of what came from those five. Analysts writing before that sale put VinFast at above a fifth of what a services side earned.

Here is what makes it worth setting down. VinFast had sold its own slips in America in August 2023. By November 2023, weeks before this company's own sale opened, VinFast's slips had fallen by above ninety in each hundred from their peak. Analysts wrote about it before bidding closed. Offer papers named VinFast as a main customer. None of this was hidden from anybody.

Buyers bid 69 times what was on offer and paid ₹1,200 to 1,400 on a first day, against ₹500 allotted.

Then a named risk arrived, on schedule.

Brokers reckon that without VinFast a rest grew near fifteen in each hundred in FY25, so work elsewhere held up. What fell away was one customer named in offer papers as a main one.

And losing him made a second named risk worse, not better. VinFast was an outside customer. With him gone, a share of work coming from a parent and a parent's British arm rose. A company that told buyers it was reducing dependence on its own family became more dependent on them. In a quarter to September 2026, a largest customer was struck by a cybersecurity incident and contributed nothing for part of that quarter.

So a shape here is not a hidden risk. It is a named one. Offer papers named VinFast. VinFast's own slips had fallen above ninety in a hundred before bidding closed. Analysts wrote it down. And a market bid 69 times over and paid 140 in a hundred above an allotted price on a first day. Whoever bought at ₹1,200 that morning was not deceived about what could go wrong. They were told, in writing, and paid anyway. That is a different complaint from concealment, and in some ways a worse one, because no rule can fix it.

Worth holding beside it: buyers who came in six weeks earlier at about ₹402 a slip faced a same disclosed risk, and left at ₹1,014.

A parent has moved little since that sale, which settles one thing: this is not a family selling down a company it built. A parent here is itself a listed company, holding steady above half.

What has moved is everyone who came in before a public did. TPG, who paid about ₹402 a slip in October 2023, sold down and was gone within eighteen months. Alpha TC Holdings, gone. Tata Capital Growth Fund, gone. Every holder who bought before that November sale has left.

Who took their place? Men in town who gather villagers' monthly money. Their share went from 2.50 to 5.55 in each hundred. Foreign holders went 2.02 to 6.59. And a count of individual holders rose to above fourteen lakh by mid-2025 and has since fallen to 11.89 lakh — so above two lakh people who held slips no longer do.

TPG sold 3.65 crore slips across three days for about ₹2,935 crore. Against about ₹1,467 crore paid in October 2023 at about ₹402 a slip, that is near twice their money in eighteen months. Alpha TC took ₹1,005 crore in one day.

And a reason these all went as blocks instead of into open trading is worth naming, because it is a whole mechanism. Push 21 lakh slips into a market and a price falls while you sell — a seller pays for his own exit. Hand them to one buyer at a price agreed beforehand and a price does not move at all. What is needed is a buyer large enough to take a block without flinching, and a house gathering villagers' money every month is such a buyer by construction. Monthly money must be put somewhere. So an arrangement that lets a large holder leave without paying for his own exit is not incidental to monthly savings. Monthly savings are what makes it possible.

What is settled, and what still is not. A transfer is settled. Four fund houses paid ₹164.85 crore to one man on 4 September 2026 at ₹785 a slip. Those houses hold money villagers send in monthly, which is what a fund house is. So money left villagers' hands and reached his, and a date, a price and a count are on record for it. Two months on a slip is ₹648, so what those houses bought has fallen 17.5 per cent, near ₹28.8 crore on that block alone.

Settled too: across six months before that day a slip ran from near ₹507 to ₹891, a rise of 75.7 per cent, while profit fell in each of two quarters — 204 to 181 to 171 crore — as money coming in rose 8.1 per cent. Whatever lifted that price, reported harvests did not.

Not settled, and worth holding onto: nobody has shown any act of lifting that price, and a rise against falling harvests is a fact without an author. Nothing is known about what his slips cost him — he managed this company, he did not found it, so an amount he gained over an amount he paid cannot be worked out here. And those four houses may yet prove right; ₹785 could look cheap in ten years. A finding is not that buyers were foolish. A finding is that a transfer of this size, at a price set after a rise harvests did not support, ran from pooled monthly money into one hand.

One further oddity a reader should not be left to trip over. A quarter to December 2025 shows profit of ₹7 crore against ₹169 to 204 crore in quarters either side, with other income of minus ₹125 crore and tax at 71 per cent. Something one-off sits in that quarter and no cause is established here.

Corrected 3 November 2026, and kept in view, not edited away. A first version of this page, published hours earlier, carried four errors, and a manner of making them matters more than a fact of them.

It said FY24 figures could not be found, and used that absence to claim a fall in profit could not be dated. FY24 was available all along — ₹5,117 crore in, ₹679 crore kept — and it shows profit peaked in FY24, not FY23. A dating that was called impossible was a subtraction away.

It said no figure for cash a business generates could be obtained, and so declared a funding test un-runnable. Every such figure was available, and it comes out in this company's favour. An absence I reported was protecting a shape of my own argument.

It put a parent's holding at 53.09 per cent from a subtraction of mine. A filed figure is 55.17 per cent, and a quarterly series existed.

It also said a link between a man reducing his holding and funds raising theirs could not be established, and named a record that would settle it. That record exists and is public, and it settles it: four fund houses bought what he sold, to a single slip, at his price, on his day. A document I named as missing was one I never opened.

A cause of all four was one habit: a pattern found at another company was carried here, and searching stopped once figures fit that pattern. A register of who holds slips — where this page's most interesting column turned out to be — was never opened at all.

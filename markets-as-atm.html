ASK Automotive: Public Markets as the Family ATM

A companion to the full forensic report — how the withdrawal channel works, what keeps it sellable, and who is left holding the position while it runs.

An ATM only works if two things are true: the machine has to dispense on demand, and someone has to keep depositing into the account behind it. For the Rathee family, the deposits come from two directions at once — the company's own operating cash, drawn off in small, procedurally approved amounts, and the public market's cash, drawn off in large, unconstrained amounts — and the second channel only opened once the company listed.

Before listing, every withdrawal route had a limit built into it. A buyback can only spend cash the company actually has, is taxed at roughly 23%, and under the company's own loan covenants required lender consent. Remuneration is capped by the Companies Act. Rent has to clear an audit committee as arm's-length. Dividends have to go to every shareholder pro rata, family and public alike. Under those limits, across FY21–FY23, the family drew about ₹72 crore a year, every rupee of it out of the company's own cash. Listing didn't invent a new route. It removed the ceiling on the one route — selling shares — that had no ceiling to begin with, because the buyer on the other side was no longer the company's own bank account but the public market itself. Measured the same way, the post-listing rate is about ₹856 crore a year. The machine didn't change. What it was plugged into did.

The rent line is worth sitting with on its own, because it shows the same withdrawal machine running underneath the headline share sales, quietly, every single year. Each son collects rent from the company he co-manages, and the rent has risen every year without exception — ₹2.38 crore to ₹3.03 crore each, up 27% over six years, with FY25 at ₹2.89 crore each confirmed in the filed related-party disclosure. In FY23 each son's rent, ₹2.62 crore, was already larger than his own salary of ₹2.34 crore for running the business day to day. A separate stream, A.P. Automotives, another family-connected entity, has drawn ₹10.86 crore in rent over the same six years. And the statutory CSR obligation — a legal requirement, not a discretionary payment — is entirely, 100% of it, routed through a single implementing trust the company's own filings classify as one its directors and their relatives can significantly influence.

The ASK-9 plant is the cleanest example of how a withdrawal gets engineered to leave no trace at all. In February 2018 the company signed a nine-year lease on its own factory to its own 49%-owned joint venture. Twenty-eight days later it sold that same factory to the Chairman's wife. Three days after that, it wrote to the joint venture confirming she was now the landlord. Every related-party rent payment the company makes to a son or to A.P. Automotives shows up, dutifully, in the disclosure schedule required by law. The ASK-9 rent does not, and structurally cannot — because after March 2018 the listed company is not a party to that lease at all. It built the annuity, then handed the income stream to a promoter through a company it still controls, and stepped outside the frame the law requires it to disclose inside.

None of this drip needs public money to fund it in any direct, traceable sense — it comes out of ordinary operating cash, the same cash a private company would have. What listing changed is scale and constraint on the other channel. And what makes that other channel possible at the size it runs is that 98.9% of the shares sold into it were never paid for. Two bonus issues — 20-for-1 in March 2018, 3-for-1 in March 2021 — manufactured nearly the entire sellable pile for free, and each one lands beside an extraction event on the calendar: the 2018 bonus the day after ASK-9 was sold to the Chairman's wife, the 2021 bonus five months before the first buyback. Only 1.36 crore rupees of cash was ever subscribed for equity in this company, the last rupee of it in 2005. Everything sold since is drawn against a balance that cost the family almost nothing to create.

Which needs a buyer who still believes the growth is coming

A withdrawal channel this size only stays open if someone keeps buying at a price worth selling into, which means the growth-and-margin story has to keep looking intact from one call to the next. That requirement is what turns ordinary segment reporting into something else.

Start with the name. "Advanced Braking Systems" is the company's 36.9%-of-revenue segment, and its own product page lists what's actually in it: brake shoes, brake linings, clutch plates — friction parts for drum brakes. The annual report says so in as many words. There is no anti-lock module, no wheel-speed sensor, nothing electronic. And the word that would make that unmistakable — "anti-lock" — appears zero times in the 462-page prospectus and zero times in the 344-page FY26 annual report, even as the segment is labelled "ABS" on the investor deck's own revenue chart and in the annual report's own section heading. On at least one earnings call, an analyst's question about "the ABS segment" used the phrase to mean the friction business, in the same session where two other analysts used the identical three letters to mean the anti-lock mandate the company has no product to meet.

The wheel-assembly business is the sharper case, because the company's own prospectus proves management knew exactly what it was doing with it, five months before the IPO. The segment carried a gross spread of 1.90% in FY23 — before labour, before overhead, before depreciation — which makes it pass-through billing, not a margin business. It was also, in FY22 and FY23, the fastest-growing line in the entire company: up 42.79% and 45.87% against company-wide growth of 30.65% and 26.90% — precisely the two years of growth shown to IPO buyers at 45.6 times earnings. The prospectus then states, in June 2023, exactly what removing this segment will do to the numbers:

"We propose to convert our wheel assembly arrangements into job-work agreements in the coming years... there is no significant impact on profitability expected... certain metrics such as our PAT Margin are likely to show an improvement due to the lower profitability margins in our wheel assembly business category."

That is management telling the market, in writing, two years in advance, that a coming margin improvement will be pure arithmetic and not performance. What actually happened was not even the conversion promised — a customer pulled 60% of the business in Q4 FY25 and the rest by early FY27, described on the call as the company "requesting" and then the customer "shifting" and "taking it back." When the mechanical margin gain duly arrived, it was presented as two achievements bundled together: "improving by 150 basis points... 80 basis points will come because the wheel assembly has gone, and 70 basis points will come for the internal more efficiencies and economies of scale." The margin moved exactly 80 points — the total arriving at the number he had assigned to the mechanical half alone, with the guided 150 missed by 70. The accounts do not let anyone go further than that: management also attributes the gain to product mix, plant utilisation and an aluminium pass-through effect, and no audited bridge splits those 80 points between causes. So the shortfall is certain; the claim that the operational component specifically delivered zero is not testable and is not made here.

A December 2025 broker note still headlined this as "structural margin expansion underway, driven by mix, scale and portfolio rationalization" and set a ₹593 target — six days after the promoter's second sale. And the segment's treatment inverted the moment its direction changed: included in every growth figure while it was the fastest-growing line in the company (FY22–23), quietly stripped out of "growth ex-wheel-assembly" the moment it turned down (FY24–26). It was worth counting while it flattered the multiple the IPO was priced on, and worth excluding once it stopped.

The accounts make this nearly impossible to check independently. Every investor deck splits revenue four ways and every call quotes segment growth rates — yet the company's own quarterly filings state, under Ind AS 108: "there are no other reportable segments." One segment, on paper. Four segments, on the call. So there is no audited segment revenue or margin for the one business — aluminium lightweighting, 50.8% of revenue — that carries the disclosed ₹230 crore regulatory headwind from the anti-lock mandate. The figure needed to size the company's largest named risk is the one figure the accounting structure ensures cannot be produced.

This is the mechanism that does the actual work of thread four: the stress of making the growth story real gets pushed onto whoever is listening to the call and holding the stock, while the one commitment that never bends is the one that pays the family. Across eighteen tracked, quantified claims on eight consecutive calls — export targets, margin targets, debt targets, capex budgets, plant utilisation, a named ₹100 crore export order that shipped but was never sized or updated again — the operational promises miss, shrink, or get restated with a straight face. "Our revenue from exports remained the same at Rs. 147 crore against Rs. 174 crore last year" is, on the two numbers in that same sentence, a 15.5% decline, and no analyst on the call challenged it. The one commitment delivered early and in full, across the entire record, was the promoter's own dilution timetable — the one that moved ₹911.7 crore into his account eight months ahead of the schedule he himself had given.

So someone else has to hold the position while the story resolves

Every withdrawal has a counterparty. Someone bought each share the family sold, and what happened to them afterward is the part no earnings call ever revisits.

The two large sales show, side by side, that the family understood exactly how much the buyer's identity mattered. The first, September 2024, went into the open market at an all-time high and cost the stock 17.1% within ten sessions. The second, November 2025, went to a single buyer — SBI Mutual Fund, in a pre-arranged block — and moved the price 0.01%. Same seller, same rough quantity, and selling to a single pre-agreed buyer instead of the open market was worth an estimated ₹58 crore in avoided price impact — an estimate derived from the two sales' price behaviour, not a filed figure. The market absorbed one sale by falling and the other by not noticing, and the difference was entirely in who was on the other side of the trade.

The quarterly filings name who absorbed it, and what happened to them afterward is the sharpest evidence in this whole record of who actually waits. Three institutions appear for the first time in the quarter of the first sale, together absorbing 59% of it. One of them, Allspring Emerging Markets, has held exactly 2,559,155 shares — the identical count — for seven consecutive quarters since. Two full years, not one share traded. The SBI schemes that took the second sale did the same: frozen at 7,908,563 shares for three quarters running. Meanwhile, across the identical window, other holders — Goldman Sachs, SBI Life — kept adding, quarter after quarter. Same stock, same period: the holders who specifically absorbed a promoter sale went motionless, while everyone else kept buying. That is not a market verdict on the company. It is a verdict on the price those specific buyers paid.

Both sales also landed at what turned out, only in hindsight, to be local tops. The quarter of the first sale printed an EPS record not matched for four quarters. The quarter of the second sale tied the all-time high; the very next quarter fell 10.4% and has not recovered it since. The family's own timing, twice, coincided precisely with the moment the growth story looked its most finished — which is, mechanically, the moment a buyer is most willing to pay up for it.

That is the honest shape of "waiting for a higher bidder," and it is worth being exact about which part is unflattering. The IPO entry itself has done fine — ₹282 to roughly ₹560 by August 2026, with earnings growing faster still. The entries that struggled are the ones anchored to the family's own sale dates. The buyer at those prices is not waiting for the company to get better; the operating numbers, to the company's credit, mostly did get better. That buyer is waiting to find another buyer willing to pay more than a price the seller had already decided, twice, was the right moment to exit.

The tally, and why it isn't a rich-multiple story. Since listing, EPS has grown 142% while the price has grown roughly 99% — from the ₹282 issue price to about ₹560 in August 2026 — so the multiple has actually compressed, 45.6× to around 37×. This is not a promoter riding a rising multiple and cashing out froth; the multiple fell while ₹2,069 crore reached the family since 2021, of which ₹1,745.61 crore came from share buyers and ₹323.39 crore from company cash through buybacks, remuneration, rent, CSR and dividends. What makes a number that size possible at an unremarkable multiple is the share count sitting underneath it. Two bonus issues multiplied the count 84-fold at zero cash cost — which is not, as an earlier version of this series claimed, a way to increase an ownership percentage, since a pro-rata bonus cannot do that. What it does is collapse the cost basis to the RHP-certified ₹0.02 a share and manufacture enough units to be listable and sellable at all, against total equity cash ever subscribed of ₹1.36 crore, last paid in 2005. Ordinary arithmetic on a nearly-free pile does the same job a rich multiple would have done on a paid-for one. Whichever route gets used, the shape is identical: the seller is paid today, in full, for growth someone else still has to sit through.

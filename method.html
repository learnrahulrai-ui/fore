Standards

What these reports are built from, what they test for, and what they will not do.

Everything in a report comes from a document that anyone can open. Not a summary of a document, and not a data vendor's transcription of one. This is not fastidiousness for its own sake — in the course of one company's research, two well-known aggregators described the same promoter share sale at ₹487.9 crore and ₹516.4 crore, while the exchange filing said ₹539.5 crore. All three appeared on reputable sites. Only one was the filing.

The sources, in rough order of how much work each one repays:

SourceWhat it settles
The prospectus (DRHP/RHP)Why the company listed and who took the money. The objects clause states whether a single rupee reaches the business. The risk factors disclose customer concentration, related-party volume, litigation and regulatory exposure — and what is absent from them matters as much as what is present.
Quarterly shareholding XBRLPromoter-by-promoter holdings, by name, every quarter. This is where inter-family transfers become visible — the ones that make a large sale look like a small one at group level.
Insider and SAST disclosuresExact dates, share counts, prices and before/after holdings for every promoter transaction. Always preferred over any news report of the same event.
Audited annual accountsThe related-party schedule above all: rent, remuneration, sitting fees, purchases from group entities, guarantees, loans. Read across six years, not one.
Earnings-call transcriptsRead in sequence, not individually. A claim made in one year is checked against the accounts two years later.
Daily price and volumeWhat the market actually did on the day a promoter sold, and in the ten sessions after.

The tests

The Public Return Test. From the IPO date and from each promoter sale date, the stock's forward return is measured at six, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four and thirty-six months against an India-listed gold ETF and against real inflation of ten per cent a year, compounded to the window. Every horizon is reported, including the ones the company wins. The question that matters most is whether the answer changes depending on whether you bought before the promoter started selling or alongside him.

Said versus did. Every quantified forward claim on every call is recorded with its date and checked against what the accounts later showed. Guidance that is quietly restated downward, and then described as intact, counts as a miss. The pattern that matters is not how many promises are kept but which ones — whether the commitments that get delivered on time are the ones that pay shareholders or the ones that pay the promoter.

The language test. For each management statement, one question: does it commit to something checkable — a number, a date, a named counterparty — or does it only sound confident while committing to nothing? Fluency is not evidence of an answer. The signal is the gap: a speaker precise to the crore on twelve topics and suddenly vague on the thirteenth has told you which one is the thirteenth. Tracked across years, a topic that grows less answerable as the facts become clearer is worth more than any single evasion.

The money-flow trace. Every route by which value reaches the promoter family, followed as far back as the filings allow: share sales, buybacks, dividends, remuneration, sitting fees, rent, purchases from group entities, CSR routed to related trusts, and assets sold to relatives. Listed separately, because they are not the same thing — a buyback is extraction, a purchase of goods is trade, and a corporate guarantee to a wholly-owned subsidiary is neither. The report says which is which.

Incremental return on new capital. Headline return ratios describe plants built decades ago and long since depreciated. What a buyer today is funding is the new capital, so incremental profit is measured against incremental capital expenditure and compared with the company's own cost of debt. The two numbers are frequently very far apart.

What these reports will not do

No price targets. No buy, hold or sell. No forecast of what any share will do next. No modelled earnings for years that have not happened. Those are the parts of conventional research that are least verifiable and most easily shaded, and their absence is deliberate.

What replaces them is a record of what already happened, stated plainly enough to be checked and, if wrong, corrected. Four further rules follow from that.

Uncertainty is stated in the sentence, not the footnote. If a figure could not be verified, the report says so where the figure appears. Several items in the published research remain open — a plant's sale price, an amalgamation's consideration, a rent that sits in a joint venture's accounts rather than the company's — and each is named as unresolved rather than estimated into apparent completeness.

Findings that fail get published too. A pattern that looks damning and then turns out to be ordinary is reported as ordinary. A company that beats its guidance is credited with beating it. A promoter who discloses something awkward voluntarily is credited with disclosing it.

Corrections stay on the page. Where an earlier version of a report was wrong, the correction is visible in the published text rather than silently edited out. The one significant error so far — a stake movement dated after a listing when the filings showed it happened before — is described in the report itself, because a reader has no way to judge the rest of the work without seeing how mistakes are handled.

Nothing is asserted about intent. Filings establish what was done, when, by whom and for how much. They do not establish why. Where a sequence of disclosed, individually legal steps produces a result that consistently favours one party, the report sets out the sequence and the arithmetic and lets the reader draw the conclusion. Every step in the research published so far was disclosed by the company itself. None of it was hidden. It had simply never been assembled in one place.

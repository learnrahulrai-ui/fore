On a payment law compels, and the single choice it leaves open

ASK Automotive Limited, FY21 to FY26, worked from filings a company lodged itself. August 2026.

Open a set of accounts for any company controlled by one household and you will find money running from that company to those who control it. Rent for ground. Pay for holding a post. Money for goods bought from a firm kin own. Money for buying back pieces from a chairman.

Now, each of those has a defence, and I want to grant every defence its full weight before we start, because an argument that has not first granted an opponent his best case is worth nobody's afternoon. Ground does earn rent, and somebody owns that ground. A man running eighteen plants works a long day and may draw pay for it. Brake linings bought from a kinsman's firm arrive on a lorry, and they are real linings. A board weighing spare cash against uses may choose, on sound grounds, to buy back pieces. Grant all of it. None of what follows disturbs any of it.

There is one payment in that same ledger which carries no defence at all, and I want you to be careful about why, because a reason is not what a reader expects. It is not that some defence fails under examination. It is that no defence is called for, since nothing was decided.

Section 135 of India's Companies Act compels a company past a certain size to lay out near two rupees in each hundred of average earnings across three prior years on public good. Compelled, note, in three respects: that money leave, how much leaves, and that a yearly report name each project. A board declining must set down its reasons in writing.

One question, and one alone, stays with a board: who receives it.

So we have an instrument, and I use that word with intent. Every other channel in those accounts mixes a household's preference together with a business judgement, and no reading can pull those two apart afterwards — that is why arguments about rent and pay never settle. Here there is no business judgement to mix in. Money was leaving whatever anybody wanted. What remains, once you strip out everything compelled, is preference on its own, uncontaminated.

Work it paid for, as a yearly report sets out: training for work at Jhajjar; schooling at Gurgaon, Rewa, Ghaziabad, Prayagraj and Delhi; village sport at Faridabad, Sonipat and Bhiwani; care of land and air at Gurgaon. Nine towns, four kinds of work, six years.

Now count carriers. A body receiving money of this kind must register once with a central registry, after which a number follows it about, shaped as CSR and eight digits. Count how many distinct numbers appear across all nine towns and all four kinds of work.

One.  —  CSR00000244, AHSAAS.

Not a majority of it. Not a large share. All of it, for six years, through one pair of hands, with nothing anywhere in any filing to suggest a second carrier was ever weighed against a first.

Whose hands those are wants no inference from us, and I would not offer one. Two documents a company lodged itself answer that in plain words. From a filing sent to an exchange:

"born out of chairman & MD Kuldip Singh Rathee and his wife"

And from a schedule of payments to kin, which every listed company must publish, naming money reaching persons and bodies connected to whoever runs it. AHSAAS appears there, under a heading covering bodies:

"in which directors of the Company and their relatives are able to exercise significant influence"

A duty owed to strangers, discharged through a body a chairman and his wife founded, which a company's own accountants file under connected parties. Both statements published. Neither buried. They sit in separate documents, which is all that has ever kept them apart.

I should say, in plain terms, that up to here I have shown you nothing new, and nothing that ought to trouble a careful person much. Household foundations are common across most trading nations. A founder watching a thing that carries his name may well run it better than a hired stranger would. Were this all there is, we should close the file. What comes next does not depend on anybody's view about foundations, and it is not a matter of opinion at all.

Does that formula admit these six figures?

Read that row once more as any reader would read it: six yearly sums a company gave away under a legal duty. If that reading is right, then each figure equals two in each hundred of a mean of three prior years' earnings. That is what a statute says, and a statute is not vague about it.

Good. A formula that fixes a figure can be run backwards. Divide by two in each hundred — which is to say, multiply by fifty — and each payment hands back a mean it must have come from. Let us do that for all six and see what we are asked to believe.

Read down that middle column: fifty-one, then a hundred ninety-five, then thirty-five, then sixty-five, then a hundred eighty-six, then two hundred twenty-two.

Anybody who has handled a moving mean will already be uneasy, and I want to name a reason for it, and not leave it as a feeling. Two windows standing next to each other share two of their three years. Only one year enters and one year leaves. So consecutive means are tied to one another by construction — they may drift, they cannot leap. And earnings at this company rose across our whole stretch without a break: 82.66 crore in FY22, 122.95 in FY23, 247.62 in FY25, 297.32 in FY26. A moving mean of a rising series rises.

Let us take a sharpest pair and put it on a board, because a feeling of unease settles nothing and arithmetic settles it in five lines.

Let P19, P20, P21, P22 stand for earnings in those four years. (1)  FY22 payment = 3.89 = two in each hundred of mean(P19, P20, P21)
      so mean = 3.89 × 50 = 194.5,  and  P19 + P20 + P21 = 583.5

(2)  FY23 payment = 0.71 = two in each hundred of mean(P20, P21, P22)
      so mean = 0.71 × 50 =  35.5,  and  P20 + P21 + P22 = 106.5

(3)  Subtract (2) from (1). P20 and P21 stand in both, so both cancel:

          P19 − P22  =  583.5 − 106.5  =  477.0

(4)  P22 is audited, and is not in dispute:  P22 = 82.66

(5)  Therefore  P19 = 477.0 + 82.66 = 559.66

Now look at what line (5) asks of us. It says that in FY19 these works earned 559.66 crore.

Their best year on record is FY26, at 297.32 crore. Line (5) wants FY19 — six years earlier, at a fraction of present size, on a fraction of present takings, before two of a company's plants were built — to have earned 1.88 times what these works have managed in their best year since.

That did not occur. So a reading we began with, that this row records a statutory duty, cannot be sustained, and we may set it down.

I want to stress that nothing in those five lines rests on judgement. There is no view of anybody's character in it, no inference about motive, nothing a reasonable man might weigh one way and his neighbour another. Two figures a company filed, put through a formula written into a statute, yield a third figure a company's own audited accounts forbid. A student may check every line in four minutes.

So what is it a record of? It is a record of payments into a carrier. Not spending by that carrier, and not an obligation. Three separate events, and everything of interest lives in gaps between them.

Consider both ends of a bargain. Pay an unrelated body and payment follows work, because that body answers to a board of its own, keeps accounts of its own, and has neither reason nor appetite to sit on your cash across years. Money moves against milestones because both parties want it moved so. Pay a body your own household founded and none of that holds. Timing falls to you. A smooth duty may be discharged through uneven transfers into a pot already under household hands, and a pot carries a balance from one year into a next.

Which is why a total wants setting beside a shape. Across six years these works paid 15.08 crore into AHSAAS. For a company whose earnings climbed from tens of crore toward three hundred, a duty at two in each hundred of a moving mean would come to something near that across six years. There is no sign of underpayment here and I allege none. What fails is not an amount. It is a shape — and shape is where discretion leaves its mark.

Two readings survive that, and I will not pretend otherwise.

A first is innocent. From 2021 a change in rules compelled unspent sums into a ring-fenced account, and permitted work called ongoing to hold money three years. Multi-year projects, catching up after a thin year, or a transfer made in one year against spending across several would each produce this shape, and each is lawful and unremarkable.

A second is that uneven timing was a choice, available because a carrier was one a household founded.

Nothing on this page settles that. A document settles it, and a short one: an annexure splitting amount laid out from amount transferred, year by year, together with AHSAAS's own filed accounts showing what a trust held and what it spent. Both exist. Neither has been pulled here. Until one is, what we have is a question a board answers in a paragraph, which nobody has put in six years of calls.

Who has been bearing this cost since November 2023?

A second matter, and this one turns on a date, not on arithmetic.

Before November 2023 one household owned these works outright. Money paid into a trust that household founded was, in plain economic terms, a household funding its own trust. Nobody outside had standing to mind, for nobody outside was paying.

In November 2023 strangers bought pieces. Since then a share of every cost these works carry — and this is a cost — falls upon people who are not of that household.

Students tell me at this point that two crore is a small sum, and they are right. It is small. Size is not what we are examining. What we are examining is that a channel which used to be a household paying into a pot of its own has become other people paying into a pot of that household's — and that no decision was ever taken to bring that about. A listing brought it about, and a figure climbs each year as their share climbs.

Set this channel now against every other route by which money reached that household across those same six years. Its size answers one question. Its nature answers a better one.

Smallest of four, and most telling of four, and I hope by now you see why those two go together, and do not pull against one another. Every row above it can be argued over on business grounds, and reasonable men will argue. A last row cannot, because it holds no business grounds to argue about. A statute fixed spending. A single open question was who ought to hold it. Six years running, an answer came back a same way, and no competing answer appears in any document a reader can obtain.

A word on geography before we finish, offered without complaint. Of nine towns funded, five lie in Haryana — Jhajjar, Gurgaon, Faridabad, Sonipat, Bhiwani — and these works stand at Gurugram, in Haryana. Four others fall across Delhi, and Rewa and Prayagraj beyond. Rules invite spending in a local area, and this is what local looks like when a rule is followed. Whether any of those nine carries a government marking as most in need may be checked against a published list, and has not been checked here.

Not one of those is an accusation, and not one requires an answer that reflects poorly on anybody. Each has a short, dull reply, most likely a reassuring one. That is what makes six years of silence worth recording — silence proves nothing, but nobody asked, and a reason nobody asked is that putting this page together meant reading two documents nobody reads together.

Let me set down, then, what has been shown and what has not. Shown: that a row of six figures cannot be a record of a statutory duty, since running a statutory formula backwards demands earnings in FY19 of 559.66 crore against a best year on record of 297.32 crore. That such a row therefore records payments into a carrier, and that timing of payments into a carrier falls to a payer when a payer founded it. That above two crore belonging to persons outside that household has passed into that carrier since November 2023. And that a single question a statute left open was answered a same way six years running with no competing answer on record.

Not shown, and not alleged: that a rupee went astray. That schooling at six towns failed to happen. That any step broke a rule. That anybody intended anything whatever. An auditor signed these accounts without qualification, and I have no quarrel with him.

Those two paragraphs are not a hedge. They are a boundary, and a value of everything above depends upon holding it.

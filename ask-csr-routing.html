One outflow where nothing was optional except who received it. So where did it go?

ASK Automotive Limited, FY21 to FY26, from its own filings. A ledger of 15.08 crore, a single registry number, and a row of figures that arithmetic says cannot mean what it appears to mean. August 2026.

Every payment a household draws out of works it controls carries a defence, and each defence is reasonable. Rent? Works stand on ground somebody owns, and ground earns. Pay? Somebody runs eighteen plants and a day is long. Buying back pieces? A board weighed cash against uses and chose. Goods bought from a household supplier? Those goods arrived, and brake linings are real.

One outflow carries no such defence, for a reason worth stating with care: it was never a decision to spend. Law compelled spending. Section 135 of India's Companies Act sets an amount by formula — near two rupees in each hundred of average earnings across three prior years — and a board that skips it must explain itself in writing.

Which makes this outflow a clean instrument, and nothing else in these accounts is one. Every other channel mixes a household's preference with a business rationale, so no reading of it can separate them. Here there is no rationale to mix in. Money was leaving whatever anyone preferred. A single decision remained: who receives it.

So if you want to know what a household does when its business judgement is taken out of a question and only its preference is left, this is where you look. What did six years of that preference produce?

Work it paid for, as a yearly report lists it: training for work in Jhajjar; schooling in Gurgaon, Rewa, Ghaziabad, Prayagraj and Delhi; village sport in Faridabad, Sonipat and Bhiwani; care of land and air in Gurgaon. Nine towns, four kinds of work, six years.

Now count carriers. A body receiving this money must register once with a central registry and gets a number that follows it after, shaped as CSR and eight digits. Count distinct numbers across every project across all of it:

One.   CSR00000244 — AHSAAS.

Not a majority. Not a large share. All of it, across four kinds of work in nine towns spread over three states, through one pair of hands, for six years, with no sign anywhere in any filing that a second carrier was ever weighed.

Whose hands those are calls for no inference, no registry search and no guessing from surnames. Two documents ASK filed itself answer it in plain words. A filing lodged with an exchange describes that trust as:

"born out of chairman & MD Kuldip Singh Rathee and his wife"

And a list of payments to kin — a schedule every listed business must publish, naming money reaching people and bodies connected to whoever runs it — carries AHSAAS inside it, under a heading covering bodies:

"in which directors of the Company and their relatives are able to exercise significant influence"

So a duty owed to strangers gets discharged through a body a chairman and his wife brought into being, which this business's own accountants file under connected parties. Both statements are published. Neither is buried. They sit in separate documents, and few readers hold both at once.

That much has been reported before, here and elsewhere, and on its own it proves little. Family foundations are ordinary across much of a trading world, and a founder watching a thing carrying his name may run it better than a stranger would. What follows has not been reported, and it does not depend on anyone's view about foundations.

Can that row of figures mean what it appears to mean?

Read that row again as a reader would: six yearly amounts a company gave away under a legal duty. If that reading is right, each figure equals two in each hundred of a mean of three prior years' earnings.

Which makes it testable, because a formula runs both ways. Divide by two in each hundred — multiply by fifty — and each payment hands back an implied three-year mean of earnings. Do it:

Look at what those implied means do: 51, then 195, then 35, then 65, then 186, then 222.

A mean of three years cannot behave so. Two consecutive three-year windows share two of their three years, so consecutive means are locked together — they can drift, they cannot leap. And ASK's earnings across this stretch rose without a break: 82.66 crore in FY22, 122.95 in FY23, 247.62 in FY25, 297.32 in FY26. A rolling mean of a rising series rises.

Push on a sharpest pair and it stops being an oddity and becomes impossible.

Take FY22 and FY23.

FY22 paid 3.89 → mean of FY19, FY20, FY21 = 194.5 → those three sum to 583.5
FY23 paid 0.71 → mean of FY20, FY21, FY22 =  35.5 → those three sum to 106.5

Both windows contain FY20 and FY21. Subtract one from another and those two cancel, leaving one line:

FY19 earnings − FY22 earnings = 583.5 − 106.5 = 477.0

ASK's FY22 earnings are audited at 82.66 crore. So FY19 earnings would have to be 559.66 crore.

ASK's best year on record is FY26, at 297.32 crore. That reading demands FY19 — six years before, at a fraction of present size, on a fraction of present takings — earned 1.88 times what these works have earned in their best year since.

It did not. So that row is not a record of an obligation.

Nothing in that turns on judgement, and no view about anybody is needed to follow it. Two numbers a company filed, put through a formula written into law, produce a third number a company's own audited accounts rule out.

So if a row does not record what a business owed, what does it record?

It records payments into a carrier. Not spending by that carrier, and not an obligation. Those are three separate events, and a gap between them is where this whole matter lives.

Consider both ends. Pay an unrelated body and payment tracks work: that body answers to its own board, keeps its own accounts, and has neither reason nor appetite to sit on your cash for years. Money moves against milestones because both sides want it so. Pay a body your own household founded and none of that holds. Timing becomes yours. A smooth duty can be discharged through lumpy transfers into a pot already under household hands, and a pot holds a balance across years.

Which is why a six-year total is worth setting beside a yearly pattern:

A total looks about right. A pattern cannot be. Across FY21 to FY26 these works paid 15.08 crore into AHSAAS. For a business whose earnings climbed from tens of crore toward 300, a duty at two in each hundred of a rolling three-year mean would total somewhere close to that across six years. So there is no sign of underpayment, and none is alleged. What fails is not an amount. It is a shape — and shape is what tells you where discretion sat.

Two readings survive that, and honesty needs both named.

One: bunching is innocent. From 2021 a rule change compelled unspent sums into a ring-fenced account and permitted work called ongoing to hold money for three years. Multi-year projects, catch-up after a thin year, or a transfer made in one year against spending across several would each produce this same shape, and each is lawful and common.

Two: bunching is a choice a household made because a carrier it founded let it make one.

Nothing on this page decides between those two. A document does, and it is a short one: an annexure splitting amount spent from amount transferred, year by year, together with AHSAAS's own filed accounts showing what a trust held and what it laid out. Both exist. Neither has been pulled here. Until one is, this stands as a question a board can answer in a paragraph and has not been asked in six years of calls.

Who was paying for this, and did that change?

Now a second thing an obligation cannot explain, and it turns on a date rather than on arithmetic.

Before November 2023 this business was owned outright by one household. Money it paid into a trust that household founded was, in every economic sense, a household funding its own trust. Nobody outside had standing to mind, because nobody outside was paying.

In November 2023 strangers bought pieces. Since then a share of every expense these works carry — and this is an expense — falls on people who are not that household.

So above two crore of strangers' money has passed into a trust a chairman and his wife founded, across three years, and a yearly figure climbs as their share climbs. Set against sums elsewhere in these accounts it is small. Set against what it demonstrates it is not, because a channel that used to be a household paying into its own pot has become other people paying into a household's pot, and no decision was ever taken to make that so. Listing did it.

Payments into that trust also rose across that same seam — 5.62 crore across FY21 to FY23, 9.46 crore across FY24 to FY26, a climb of 68 in each hundred. Earnings rose too, so a formula explains much of that and this is not put forward as a finding. It is put forward because a pot grows as works grow, needing no fresh decision from anyone, forever.

Set this channel against every other route by which money reached that household across those same six years, and its size answers one question while its nature answers a better one:

Smallest of four, and a most revealing of four. Every other row can be argued about on business grounds, and reasonable people will. This row cannot, because there are no business grounds in it — spending was fixed by statute and a single question left open was who should hold it. Six years running, an answer came back a same way, and a competing answer was never named in any document a reader can find.

One further pattern sits in geography and is recorded without complaint. Of nine towns funded, five lie in Haryana — Jhajjar, Gurgaon, Faridabad, Sonipat, Bhiwani — and these works themselves stand at Gurugram, in Haryana. Remaining four spread across Delhi, and Rewa and Prayagraj beyond. Law invites spending in a local area and this is what local looks like. Whether any of those nine carries a government marking as most in need is checkable against a published list and has not been checked here.

None of those is an accusation and none needs an answer that casts anyone in a poor light. Each has a short, boring, likely reassuring reply. That is why an absence of any reply across six years of calls is itself worth recording — not because silence proves a thing, but because nobody asked, and a reason nobody asked is that assembling this page took reading two documents nobody reads together.

What this page claims, stated so it can be checked. That a row of six figures cannot be a record of a statutory duty, because inverting a formula written into law demands earnings in FY19 of 559.66 crore against a best year on record of 297.32 crore. That such a row is therefore a record of payments into a carrier, and that timing of payments into a carrier is a thing a payer controls when a carrier is one it founded. That above two crore of money belonging to people outside a household has passed into that carrier since November 2023. And that a single question left open by a compelling statute — who receives — was answered a same way six years running with no competing answer on record.

What this page does not claim. That a rupee went astray. That schooling in six towns did not happen. That any step broke a rule. That anybody intended anything. An auditor signed these accounts without objection and this page has no quarrel with that.

Every figure and every quoted passage comes from documents ASK Automotive filed itself: yearly reports FY21 through FY26, sections on money given away and lists of payments to kin; audited group accounts FY21 through FY26, signed without objection by Walker Chandiok & Co LLP; a filing lodged with BSE describing AHSAAS. Payments into AHSAAS add to 15.08 crore across six years. Earnings quoted are audited group figures: FY22 82.66 crore, FY23 122.95 crore, FY25 247.62 crore, FY26 297.32 crore. A duty under Section 135 is computed on earnings under Section 198 on a standalone basis, which differ from group figures quoted here — that difference narrows a proof above by some margin and cannot close a gap of 477 crore, since no plausible basis turns 82.66 crore into 559.66.

You own all of it, and it already makes money. So why sell me a piece?

Working out what a promoter is optimising for, then checking every prediction against one company's filings — ASK Automotive Limited, 1988 to 2026. August 2026.

Sit in the chair for a minute, because nothing here makes sense from outside it.

You are in your sixties. You started making brake shoes near Gurugram in 1989 and you now own, outright, eighteen plants and roughly half of the Indian market for two-wheeler friction braking. No venture fund ever gave you money. No private equity investor sits on your board waiting to get out. The last time anybody paid cash into this company in exchange for shares was 2005, and the person who paid it was you, and the whole of it — every rupee of equity capital ever subscribed since 1988 — came to ₹1.36 crore.

The business is profitable. Nobody is forcing you into anything. So work out why you would list it.

The textbook answer is that a company sells shares to raise money it needs. That answer dies on contact with your own bank statement: you do not need it, and in fact when you do list, in November 2023, the company will receive nothing — the entire offer is existing shares sold by you and your wife, and the share count is identical the day before and the day after. The other answer, the one about letting the public participate in India's growth story, is not an answer. It is a press release.

So ask the narrow version instead, which is the only version with a real answer:

What can you do the day after listing that you could not do the day before?

To get there you have to lay out every way money can move from a company you own into your own hands, and be honest about what limits each one. There are six.

The way outWhose money it isWhat stops you doing more of it
Salary and commission to family members holding executive titlesThe company'sThe Companies Act caps managerial pay against net profit. Going past it needs a shareholder vote and a disclosure.
Rent, where the family personally owns land the company operates onThe company'sMust be arm's-length and cleared by the audit committee. A rent that jumps has to be defended.
DividendThe company's, after taxStrictly per share. The moment outsiders exist you cannot pay only yourself.
Buyback of your own sharesThe company'sLimited by cash actually available, taxed at roughly 23%, and under your loan covenants it needs the lender's permission. Banks dislike cash leaving a borrower.
Purchases and job-work routed via a family-owned supplierThe company'sRelated-party disclosure, the auditor, and transfer-pricing rules.
Selling sharesThe buyer'sNothing at all — except that in a private company there is no buyer

Read the last column downward and the shape of your problem appears. Five of the six routes drain the company's own account, which is exactly why the statute, the auditor and the lender each have standing to object to them. They are capped because they are visible, and they are visible because the money passes through the company's books on its way to you.

The sixth has no cap and never did, because the money never touches the company. It moves from a buyer straight to you. Nobody has standing to object because no company transaction has occurred. But in a private firm that route is theoretical: there is no buyer, no price, and a stake in an unlisted family business is worth whatever a rare negotiated discount says it is worth, once a decade if ever.

Which answers the question. A listing does not invent a new way out. It takes the one route that was never capped and supplies the single thing it was missing — a permanent, liquid, daily-priced crowd of buyers, valuing your company at whatever the most optimistic of them is willing to pay. Afterwards you can convert ownership into cash on any morning you choose, in any size the market will absorb, without company cash, without a lender's consent, without a statutory ceiling, and without the auditor having any view, because it is not a company transaction. It appears in no financial statement the company publishes.

That last line is worth sitting with, because it is why almost nobody sees this. A rent paid to a relative shows up in the related-party note. A buyback shows up in the cash-flow statement. A salary shows up in the remuneration table. Someone selling ₹500 crore of stock shows up in none of them, because the company did not pay it. It surfaces in a quarterly shareholding filing and a bulk-deal record — two places nobody reading an annual report ever opens.

Now keep sitting in the chair, because several sharper rules fall out of the same arithmetic, and each one predicts something specific you would go and do.

Your family is one economic unit spread across many legal persons. The law imagines a shareholder as an individual. You do not. You are a father, a wife, two sons, possibly an HUF, a few private companies and a trust — and every disclosure threshold in the system is written per legal person while your actual economics run per family. Gifts between close relatives are tax-free in India. So value can be moved between your own pockets at no cost, and every move resets what a screening tool sees. If you are about to sell a very large number of shares, and you would prefer the shareholding pattern not to show a very large number moving, you already know what to do the quarter before.

The obligation to disclose attaches to a relationship, not to a flow of money. This one is worth reading twice. The related-party schedule requires the company to be a party to a transaction. It does not require the company to have created the transaction. So if the company originates an income stream and then steps out of the contract, the stream carries on and the duty to disclose it dies. Nothing has to be concealed. The paperwork simply stops applying.

The share count is yours to set; the ownership percentage is not. A bonus issue hands every holder free shares in proportion to what they already have, out of reserves the business genuinely earned. Because it is strictly proportional it cannot change anybody's percentage — 40% before a 20-for-1 bonus is 40% after. Anyone who tells you a bonus took a promoter from 2% to 50% has made an arithmetic mistake. What it does change is the cost basis per share, which collapses, and the number of units in existence, which is the thing that decides whether the company can be listed at all. A company with 42,353 shares cannot have an IPO. There is no way to spread 42,353 units across thousands of retail applicants, no sensible per-share price, no way to meet lot and free-float rules. Before any family firm can be sold to the public its share count has to be multiplied by orders of magnitude, and a bonus does that at zero cash cost. It is not the taking. It is the thing that has to happen first, for free, so that taking becomes possible later.

Rent runs the other way from how people assume. A normal company owns its factory and pays no rent. In the version you would build, the family owns the land personally and the company rents it — which turns one asset into two income streams pointing the same way: the payment is a company expense, so it lowers the company's taxable profit, and it is family income, so it arrives without any of the caps that sit on salary. It is disclosed, honestly, in the related-party note. It is approved, properly, by an audit committee that a board you control appointed. And rent escalates automatically, every year, forever, which no salary does. The tell that this is not incidental is simple and you should look for it: compare what a family member earns as rent against what the same person earns for the executive job they actually hold. If the landlord side is bigger, you have learned which of the two is the real arrangement.

The pipe out of the country is narrow for a person and wide for a company. If a child has emigrated, moving family wealth to them personally runs into FEMA's Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which caps an individual's outward remittance at a modest figure each year. But a company's investment into an overseas subsidiary or joint venture runs under the Overseas Direct Investment rules, which permit multiples of net worth. So the vehicle that goes abroad is corporate, not personal — and once a foreign entity exists it can employ the relative at a salary nobody in India audits, buy an office the family occupies, or sit in the middle of a transfer-pricing arrangement where the Indian parent sells low and the foreign arm resells at the real price, leaving the margin outside the country. One detail matters more than the rest: that relative is generally not made a key managerial person of the Indian listed entity, because SEBI's related-party machinery keys off Indian-entity status. Skip the appointment and the foreign pay is outside the Indian company's disclosure entirely. The check that survives all of it is visible from the Indian consolidated accounts alone — capital pushed into a foreign arm over several years, set against that arm's own revenue. Large, repeated, and revenue near zero is the shape.

On this particular company, that check comes back clean and the honest thing is to say so. ASK's overseas arrangement is a joint venture with Germany's T.D. Holding GmbH in which ASK is the 49% minority and the German party holds 51% and control as technical collaborator, with about ₹2.45 crore committed. That is the opposite shape from the pattern above — a small minority stake under someone else's control is not a pipe. A separate exposure is real but is a different thing: ASK has extended roughly ₹1,021 crore of corporate guarantees and ₹479.75 crore of loans to ASK Automobiles Private Limited, a subsidiary the prospectus discloses as having negative net worth, with ₹330 crore of guarantees and ₹219.40 crore of fresh loans in FY26 alone. Because it is wholly owned, that is intra-group funding that cancels on consolidation, not money leaving for the family. It is still exposure that appears in no cash-flow statement, and public shareholders now stand behind it.

And there is never one true number. This is the quiet one, and it does more work than any of the others. Every figure you quote is picked from a family of figures that are all, individually, true. Margin on revenue, or margin on total income including other income. Debt-to-equity with lease liabilities counted, or without. Growth including a segment, or excluding it. Return on all capital ever deployed, or return on the capital deployed this year. You are not lying when you pick one. You are choosing which true thing to say — and you get to choose every quarter, and nobody announces that the basis moved.

So: if all of that is what you would work out from the chair, what would the filings of a company that had actually done it look like? Everything from here is that test. The predictions come first, the filed record second.

If you knew in 2018 that you would sell in 2023, what would you have done first?

Take the rule about disclosure attaching to a party rather than to a flow, and push on it. You own a factory. You want your wife to own it and to collect income from it, and you would rather the income not appear in the company's related-party schedule for the next nine years.

You could simply sell her the factory. But then she is a landlord with no tenant, and she has to go and negotiate a lease herself, at whatever rate a real tenant will agree, and the tenant can decline. So that is not the move.

What order would you do it in instead — and why would the order be the whole of it?

Two things fall straight out of that table, and they point in opposite directions, so both belong here.

The first is genuinely to the family's credit. Only ₹1.36 crore of cash was ever subscribed, the last of it in 2005, and everything after that was built out of retained profit. A bonus issue capitalises reserves the business actually earned; it is not a conjuring trick. Thirty-five years of compounding a brake-shoe business into an eighteen-plant group is real work and no amount of close reading changes it.

The second is the timing. The ₹0.02 certified cost basis is not a mystery to be explained — it is ₹1.36 crore divided across a count multiplied 84-fold, and the multiplication is what made a listing mechanically possible at all. But both bonus issues sit directly against other events: one the day after a plant went to the Chairman's wife, one five months before the first buyback. The pile that would be sold in 2023 was assembled first, at nil cost, and drawn on afterwards.

Which leaves the sons' transfer, and it is the cleanest confirmation in the file of the rule about the family being one unit spread across many legal persons.

Read that twice. A coming margin improvement is being described, in advance and in writing, as arithmetic with no operational content. Who is that sentence protecting, and from what — and what happens two years later when the improvement actually shows up?

Who is standing on the other side of each of these, and do they have a view about the price?

A route that depends on a buyer only stays open while the buyer keeps arriving, and buyers arrive because a story about growth still looks intact from one call to the next. So the same optimisation that shaped the share register also shapes what gets said, and the rule that does the heavy lifting is the one about there never being a single true number.

In FY22 and FY23 — the two years the prospectus showed to buyers at 45.6 times earnings — wheel assembly was the fastest-growing line in the company and nobody stripped it out of anything. From FY25 onward every growth figure quoted is the one without it. The segment counted while it flattered the number the offer was priced on, and stopped counting the moment it turned.

Then the improvement the prospectus had pre-described as arithmetic arrived, and was introduced to the market in May 2025 like this: "we will be improving by 150 basis points... 80 basis points will come because the wheel assembly has gone, and 70 basis points will come for the internal more efficiencies and economies of scale." The margin moved 80. By December 2025 a broker note had turned it into "structural margin expansion underway, driven by mix, scale and portfolio rationalization" with a ₹593 target — published six days after the promoter's second sale, on a page whose shareholding chart still read "Promoters 79%" when the filed figure was already 74.95%.

Being exact about what that does and does not show: the shortfall is certain — 150 guided, 80 delivered, and the 80 matches the component pre-disclosed in the prospectus as having no operational content. What cannot be said from these accounts is that the operational half specifically produced zero, because management also attributes the gain to product mix, plant utilisation and an aluminium pass-through effect, and no audited bridge splits those 80 points. That distinction matters and is kept.

And the exit was not a decision. In the Chairman's own words across two calls: "we were requesting the customer for the last two years to shift this business to someone else... now the customer has shifted 60% business," then "let us see once the customer takes it back." A customer removed the work. It reached the market as "portfolio rationalization."

Underneath all of it sits the reason none of this can be checked properly. Every quarterly filing states, under Ind AS 108, that there are "no other reportable segments." One segment, to the auditor. Meanwhile every investor deck splits revenue four ways and every call quotes segment growth rates. So management plainly has segment numbers and reviews them — but no audited segment revenue or margin exists for anyone outside. Including for aluminium lightweighting, which is 50.8% of revenue and carries the ₹230 crore regulatory headwind the Chairman himself named once and then declined to quantify again: "we will not talk on this hypothetical question," then "I'm sorry I can't answer on that."

Even the segment's name is doing work. The 36.9% segment is called "Advanced Braking Systems" and abbreviated on the deck's own revenue chart to "ABS" — three letters that mean anti-lock braking system to every reader in the industry. What is actually in the segment, per the company's own product page, is brake shoes, brake linings, brake pads and clutch plates: friction parts for drum brakes, no electronic control unit, no wheel-speed sensor. The formal glossary is careful and drops the S, defining "AB — Advanced Braking." The marketing layer is not. And the word "anti-lock" appears zero times in the 462-page prospectus and zero times in the 344-page FY26 annual report. It works: on a single earnings call, one analyst asked about growth in "the ABS segment" meaning friction parts, in the same session where two others used the identical three letters to mean the regulation.

So the price stays up, and the shares keep going out. Which brings the last question, the one nothing in an annual report ever answers: who took them?

And both sales landed in quarters that turned out, only afterwards, to be local earnings peaks — the September 2024 quarter printed EPS of ₹3.41, a record not exceeded for four quarters; the December 2025 quarter printed ₹4.05, tying the all-time high, after which the next quarter fell 10.4%. Timing landing twice on a high-water mark proves nothing about intent, and no claim about intent is made here. It is simply what the dates say.

There is one further thing about who buys, and it is the quiet floor under all of it. A growing share of the money arriving in a stock like this is not forming any view at all. Index funds and large-cap mandate funds fed by monthly recurring retail flows buy whatever sits in their eligible universe — index membership, enough free float, a market-cap threshold — on a fixed calendar, at whatever price the calendar finds. Which means a seller does not have to convince that money the growth story is real. He only has to keep the company checking the eligibility boxes.

Worth noticing what this whole file is not. It is not a rich-multiple story: since listing, earnings per share have grown 142% while the price grew about 99%, so the multiple compressed, 45.6× at issue to roughly 37× now. Buyers at the door were not overcharged relative to what arrived — the IPO entry is ahead. It is not a fraud: the auditor's opinion is unmodified and the business is real. And it did not need a rising multiple, because when 98.9% of the shares in issue were created free, ordinary arithmetic on a nearly costless pile does the same work a spectacular multiple would have had to do on a paid-for one.

Which leaves the only question that was ever worth asking, and it does not have one answer — it has a different answer depending on the day you walked in. Buy at the ₹282 issue price and you are ahead. Buy at ₹433 in September 2024, the week the promoter sold into the open market at an all-time high, and you watched 17.1% disappear in ten sessions and have spent much of the time since getting back. Buy at ₹473 in November 2025 and it is still too early to say, which is stated as unresolved rather than assumed. The company did not change between those three dates. What changed was whether you were buying from the market, or from him.

So: none of the above required a leak, a whistleblower, or a single document that was not published. The prospectus is 462 pages. The FY26 annual report is 344. Add eight earnings-call transcripts, twenty-four quarterly shareholding filings, six years of related-party schedules and the bulk-deal records and you are past fifteen hundred pages, every one of them free, every one of them filed by the company itself, in full compliance.

Each individual disclosure is honest. Each is also a fragment, and the chain only becomes visible when someone lays every fragment end to end and does arithmetic across documents that were never meant to be read together — comparing a certified holding in a June prospectus against a November shareholding table to find a gift nobody announced; setting a lease date against a sale date twenty-eight days later to see why the order mattered; putting a prospectus paragraph from 2023 beside an earnings call from 2025 to notice the same arithmetic being sold twice. That work is not difficult. It is just long, and it had to be paid for, and the only institutions that could afford it were the ones being paid by the company.

That was the protection. Not secrecy — assembly cost. It has just collapsed to roughly nothing, which is the actual reason a page like this exists now and did not before. The information asymmetry was never about who had the documents. Everyone had them. It was about who could afford to read all fifteen hundred pages at once and hold them in mind together.

You can now. Pull the prospectus and read the share-capital history before you read anything else. Pull every quarterly shareholding filing, not a sample, and reconcile family members against each other name by name. Read the related-party schedule for six consecutive years rather than one, and check whether any rent exceeds the salary of the person collecting it. Look for an asset sold to a relative shortly after a lease was signed on it. Note which segment carries the company's largest disclosed risk, then check whether an audited number for that segment exists at all. And when a margin improvement is announced, go back and find out whether the offer document already described it as arithmetic.

The company will keep filing everything. It always did.

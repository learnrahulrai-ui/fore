You own every part of works that already earn. Why hand parts to strangers?

Every number below comes from papers ASK Automotive Limited filed itself, 1988 to 2026. Nothing here is invented, and nothing is borrowed from a second-hand account. August 2026.

Sit where Kuldip Singh Rathee sat, and much of what follows stops being strange.

He began making brake shoes near Gurugram in 1989. By 2023 he owned eighteen works and supplied about half of what India's two-wheelers need in brake parts. No outside money-man ever bought a stake. Last cash anyone handed this business in exchange for a piece of it arrived in 2005, came to ₹1.36 crore, and came from him and his household.

Begin with a word, because everything after it depends on that word meaning one thing and not another.

A field can be divided. Split one among four sons and each holds a quarter of every harvest it will ever throw. Rathee's works were divided in that manner too, into 42,353 pieces. Hold one piece and you hold one part in 42,353 of whatever those works earn, this year and each year you keep holding. Nobody weighs it or fences it. A piece is a claim on a share of earnings, written down.

Now a second word. A man wanting to sell farmland must go and find a buyer, haggle over months, and settle for what one buyer will pay on one day. He might do it twice in a lifetime. Set against that, picture a square where buyers stand every morning with cash in hand, where a price for one piece gets called aloud and written up before noon, and where a man may sell four pieces on Tuesday and nine thousand on Thursday without asking permission from anyone. In November 2023 Rathee's works joined such a square.

Which raises a question worth sitting with before any number arrives.

His works earned. He owed no man. Nobody could force him. Why would such a man hand pieces of his works to strangers at all?

One answer gets offered so often it passes without examination: a business sells pieces to raise money it needs for growth. Test that answer against what happened. Count pieces before November 2023 and you find 197,142,600. Count pieces after and you find 197,142,600. Not one fresh piece came into being.

That count settles it, and it is worth seeing why. Money can be raised in two ways. Cut fresh pieces and sell those, and cash flows into works, works build something with it, and every existing holder's fraction shrinks a little to pay for it. Or sell pieces you hold in your own hand, and cash flows to you, while works receive nothing and no fraction shrinks except yours. Since piece-count held still, no fresh pieces were cut. So ₹833.91 crore paid by strangers that November went to two people: Kuldip Singh Rathee took ₹583.74 crore, and his wife, Mrs. Vijay Rathee, took ₹250.17 crore. His works took nothing.

Growth money, then, cannot be an answer, because no growth money changed hands. Ask a narrower question instead, since a narrower question has a real answer:

What could this man do on a Friday that he could not do on Thursday?

To reach an answer, lay out every road by which money can travel from works a man owns into that man's own hands. Six roads exist. What matters is not that they exist but what blocks each one.

Read down that last column. Five roads carry works' own money, which is why a law, an auditor and a lender each get a say. They are blocked because they are watched, and they are watched because money passes through works' books on its way out.

A sixth road carries a buyer's money. No rupee leaves works. No auditor has standing, because works did not pay anything. No lender is asked, because no loan term touches it. No ceiling applies, because no law caps what a man may charge for what he owns. That road was open all along. It had no traffic for one reason: no buyer stood at either end.

So joining that square built no new road. It put buyers on a road that had none — buyers who arrive each morning, who price a piece before noon, and who will take as many pieces as a man cares to hand over. From that Friday on, this man could turn ownership into cash on whatever morning suited him, at whatever size a square would swallow, without works' cash, without a lender saying yes, without a ceiling, and without an auditor holding a view. What follows from that last point matters more than any figure on this page: money moving down that road appears in no account works publish.

Watch how sharp that edge is. Rent paid to a son gets written into a list works must publish. Works buying back pieces shows up in a statement of cash. Pay to family sits in a table with each name against each amount. A man selling ₹372 crore of pieces on a Tuesday shows up in none of them, because works paid nothing. It surfaces in a filing about who holds what, and in a record of large trades — two places no reader of a yearly report ever opens.

Keep sitting in that chair, because four further rules fall out of what has already been laid down, and each one tells you what such a man would go and do next.

A household is one purse held in many hands. Every rule about who must declare what gets written against a single named person. A household does not work that way — a father, a wife, two sons, a trust, and a few private firms hold between them what a village would call one family's wealth. In India a gift between close kin carries no tax. So wealth shifts between pockets of one household at no cost, and each shift resets what a reader watching one name would see. If a man means to shed a great many pieces, and would sooner no reader watch a great many pieces move, he knows what to arrange in whatever quarter comes first.

A duty to declare fastens onto who signs, not onto where money goes. This one repays slow reading. Works must publish payments works make to kin. That duty catches works while works remain a party to an arrangement. It says nothing about arrangements works created and then stepped out of. Create an income and hand over your seat at that table, and income carries on flowing while duty to publish it dies with your seat. Nothing gets hidden. Paperwork stops reaching.

Piece-count bends. Fractions do not. Hand every holder more pieces in proportion to what each already holds, taking no payment, and no holder's fraction moves at all — two fifths before is two fifths after, since everyone grew alike. Cutting one field into twenty strips gives a man no more ground. What bends instead is cost for each piece, which collapses, and how many pieces exist, which decides whether pieces can be sold to strangers at all. Nobody can sell 42,353 pieces to thousands of buyers. Prices land at absurd sizes, lots cannot be cut, and rules about how much must sit in public hands cannot be met. Before any household business can be handed to a square, its piece-count has to grow by a great deal, and handing out free pieces does that without a rupee. It is not a taking. It is what has to happen first, for nothing, so that a taking can happen later.

A door out of a country stands narrow for a person and wide for a firm. If a child has gone abroad, sending household wealth to that child as a person runs into a yearly ceiling on what one individual may remit. A firm sending money into a business it owns abroad runs under a different rule altogether, one that permits several times what a firm is worth. So a vehicle going abroad is a firm, never a person. Once a firm exists abroad it can pay a relative a wage nobody in India examines, buy a house called an office, or sit between a seller and a buyer so that profit settles outside a country's reach. One detail decides whether any of it gets published: that relative is kept off any post in India's own listed works, since duty to declare fastens onto India posts. Skip that post, and wages abroad sit outside what works must write down. A reader can still check, from India accounts alone: money pushed into a business abroad across several years, set against what that business earns. Much pushed in, little earned, year after year, is a shape worth naming.

For these works that check comes back clean, and saying so matters more than scoring a point. ASK's arrangement abroad is a venture with Germany's T.D. Holding GmbH in which ASK holds 49 parts in 100 and Germans hold 51 along with control, on a commitment near ₹2.45 crore. A minority stake under another party's hand is not a door out. A second exposure is real, though it is a different animal: ASK has stood behind ₹1,021 crore of guarantees and ₹479.75 crore of loans to ASK Automobiles Private Limited, a business ASK's own offer papers describe as worth less than nothing, with ₹330 crore of guarantees and ₹219.40 crore of fresh loans in FY26 alone. Since ASK owns all of it, that is money moving within one group, not money leaving for a household. It remains an exposure that shows in no statement of cash, and strangers holding pieces now stand behind it.

And no figure has one true version. This rule does more work than every other, and it hides behind arithmetic that offends nobody. Ask what portion of money coming in stays as earnings, and an answer depends on whether you count money that came from lending spare cash. Ask how much a business owes against what it is worth, and an answer depends on whether rent owed on leased ground counts as owing. Ask how fast it grew, and an answer depends on whether a line being wound down still counts. Each version is true. A man picking among them tells no lie. He picks which true thing to say, picks afresh each quarter, and nobody announces a change of basis.

Those five rules were built from where he sat, without opening a single filing. So what would papers look like, filed by a household that had lived by them for thirty-five years? Everything below tests that. A question comes first each time. Filed papers come second.

If you meant to sell in 2023, what would you build in 2018?

Take a rule about duty fastening onto who signs, and lean on it. You own ground with works standing on it. You want your wife to own that ground and draw income from it, and you want that income kept out of a list works must publish for years to come.

Selling her that ground on its own fails. She becomes a landowner holding an empty yard. She must go and find a tenant herself, argue rent with a man free to walk away, and accept whatever a real bargain yields.

In what order would you arrange it instead — and why would order be everything?

Words inside quotation marks on this page stand as filed, and carry language this page avoids elsewhere. Read that admission twice. A coming improvement is described in advance, in writing, as arithmetic with no work behind it. Who does putting that in writing protect, and against what — and what happens two years on when that improvement shows up?

That line is kept.

And dropping that line was no decision. In a chairman's own words across two calls: "we were requesting the customer for the last two years to shift this business to someone else... now the customer has shifted 60% business," and later "let us see once the customer takes it back." A customer took work away. A market heard "portfolio rationalization."

Under all of it sits a reason none of this can be checked. Every quarterly filing states that no separate lines need reporting. One line, in books an auditor signs. Meanwhile every presentation to buyers cuts takings four ways and every call quotes growth for each. So management holds those figures and reviews them — while no audited figure for any line exists for anyone outside. Including for aluminium parts, at 50.8 in each 100 of all takings, carrying a ₹230 crore threat from coming brake rules that a chairman named once and then declined: "we will not talk on this hypothetical question," and after that "I'm sorry I can't answer on that."

Even a name does work. A line worth 36.9 in each 100 is called "Advanced Braking Systems" and shortened on a chart to "ABS" — three letters meaning, to every reader in this trade, a braking system that stops wheels locking. What sits inside that line, per these works' own product page, is brake shoes, brake linings, brake pads and clutch plates: friction parts for drum brakes, holding no electronics of any kind. A formal glossary drops that third letter and defines "AB — Advanced Braking." A chart aimed at buyers does not. And a word that would end all doubt, "anti-lock", appears nowhere across 462 pages of offer papers and nowhere across 344 pages of a yearly report. It works: on one call, an analyst asked about growth in "the ABS segment" meaning friction parts, during a session where two others used those three letters to mean coming rules.

So a price holds up, and pieces keep going out. Which leaves a question no yearly report answers: who took them?

Timing landing twice on a peak proves nothing about what a man meant, and no claim about what he meant gets made here. It is what dates say.

One further thing about who buys sits underneath everything. A growing share of money arriving in works like these forms no view at all. Managers gathering small monthly sums from households buy whatever sits inside a list they must track — membership of an index, enough pieces in public hands, a size threshold — on a fixed calendar, at whatever price that calendar meets. Which means a seller need not persuade that money his story is sound. He needs works to keep matching a list.

Worth marking what this file is not. It is no tale of a price running mad: since November 2023, earnings for each piece grew 142 in 100 while a price grew near 99, so a buyer today pays fewer years of earnings than a buyer at ₹282 did — forty-five years then, near thirty-seven now. Whoever paid at that door was not overcharged against what arrived. It is no fraud: an auditor signed without objection and these works are real. And it needed no wild price, because when 98.9 pieces in every 100 came into being for nothing, plain arithmetic on a costless pile does work that a wild price would have to do on a paid-for one.

Which leaves one question worth asking, and it holds no single answer. It holds a different answer for each door a man walked in through. Pay ₹282 in November 2023 and you stand ahead. Pay ₹433 in September 2024, in a week a chairman sold into open trading at a high never reached before, and you watched 17.1 in 100 vanish across ten trading days and have spent much of two years climbing back. Pay ₹473 in November 2025 and it stands unresolved, which gets said here as unresolved, not guessed. These works did not change between those three dates. What changed is whether you bought from a market, or from him.

None of what stands above needed a leak, a whistle blown, or one page nobody could obtain. Offer papers run 462 pages. A yearly report for FY26 runs 344. Add eight transcripts of calls, twenty-four quarterly filings on who holds what, six years of lists of payments to kin, and records of large trades, and you pass fifteen hundred pages — every page free, every page filed by these works, every rule obeyed.

Each filing on its own is honest. Each is also a fragment. A chain shows itself only when a man lays every fragment end to end and does arithmetic across documents nobody meant to be read together: holding a certified fraction from June against a table from November, to find a gift nobody announced; setting a tenancy date against a sale date twenty-eight days after, to see why order was everything; putting a paragraph from 2023 beside a call from 2025, to catch one piece of arithmetic sold twice.

That labour is not hard. It is long. It had to be paid for. And houses able to pay for it were houses being paid by whoever was selling.

A man walking his own fields sees every field at once, and knows in an afternoon which tenant paid and which did not. What would he have to do to know as much about works whose whole life sits across fifteen hundred pages, written in a language built for filing, not for reading?

That gap was never about who held those papers. Everyone held them. It was about who could afford to read fifteen hundred pages at once and hold them in mind together. Cost of that reading has fallen to near nothing, which is why a page like this can exist now and could not before.

So take it to whatever business you hold pieces of. Pull its offer papers and read a history of pieces before reading anything else. Pull every quarterly filing on who holds what, not a sample, and tally family members against each other name by name. Read six straight years of payments to kin, and check whether any rent runs past pay drawn by a man collecting it. Hunt for ground sold to a relative soon after a tenancy was signed over it. Mark which line carries a largest named threat, then check whether an audited figure for that line exists at all.

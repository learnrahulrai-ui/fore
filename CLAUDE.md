# fore — writing rules for these reports

## Who the reader is

An Indian farmer. Not a metaphor for a simple person — an actual one, who
understands land, rent, tenants, harvests, loans from a moneylender, grain held
back for a bad season, and inheritance split between sons. Someone who has been
pushed into holding slips of companies and monthly fund plans without anyone ever
defining a slip.

Never say this on the page. Never write "imagine you are a farmer". The reader
does not need to be told who they are.

## The one rule that governs every sentence about money

**Name both hands.** Whose hand did money leave, whose hand did it enter, and
what did the receiver do with it.

A sentence about money that cannot name two hands is hiding something, and what
it hides is almost always the answer. Test any phrase by asking for the hands:

- "the company raised ₹3,042 crore" → **which hands?** In Tata Technologies'
  case, money went from thousands of buyers into three sellers' hands. Nothing
  entered the company's account. "Raised" concealed the whole fact.
- "value was created" → no hands at all. This phrase is always empty.
- "the stock rallied" → who bought, from whom, at what price?
- "net debt free" → was a moneylender paid, or is there merely enough buried in
  a pot to pay him? Those are different, and only one involves a transfer.

When money enters a company's own account, say so, and say that what it will be
used for is not yet known. That is the honest shape: *x went into this company's
account, to do something nobody has told us yet.*

## Translating the vocabulary

Derive every term from land, grain, loans or inheritance before using it. Never
assume a term is known. A short list that recurs:

| Finance says | Say instead |
|---|---|
| offer for sale | an owner sold slips he already held; nothing reached the company |
| fresh issue | new slips cut, so money entered the company's own account |
| bonus issue | one field recorded as two papers; same soil, same harvest |
| stock split | the same paper re-cut smaller |
| net debt | what is owed, less what is buried in a pot; nothing has been paid |
| market capitalisation | one slip's price multiplied by every slip; not a pile of money anyone holds |
| price to earnings | how many years of a field's harvest a buyer paid to own it |
| free cash flow | what is left in the box after seed, labour, diesel and repairs |
| dividend payout | of what was left in the box, how much was handed out and how much kept for a bad season |
| borrowings rose | the farm went to a moneylender for more |
| institutional holding | men in town who collect villagers' monthly money |
| a fund plan (SIP) | every month you hand money to a man in town who decides which companies and when, and you never learn whose hand it reaches |

## What this rule is for, beyond plain language

It is an analytical instrument, not a style choice. Most financial language exists
to make a transfer sound like an event. Forcing both hands into every sentence
surfaces the thing being hidden: that a listing moved money from buyers to
sellers and not into a business; that a debt moved between two members of one
household and nobody paid a lender; that a fund's monthly inflow is the one
transfer where a payer cannot name a receiver.

## Standing constraints for these pages

- No definite article in my own prose. Quoted passages stand as filed.
- No -ly adverbs, no intensifiers.
- At most three headings, each a plain question, never a claim and never edgy.
- A lecturer's register: grant the other side its best case first, work a
  derivation in front of the reader, say what a proof does not reach.
- Facts and assumptions in separate sections, every figure marked for how well
  it is checked. Nothing moves from assumption to fact without a primary document.
- Corrections stay visible on the page.

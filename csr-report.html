India moves 35,000 crore a year to public good and lets whoever pays it choose where that money lands. What happens next?

A report on how compelled giving gets routed in India, built from filed aggregates and from one company's own papers. August 2026.

Start with a claim that sounds wrong and is not: India runs one compulsory payment where a payer picks who receives it.

Income tax leaves a payer and reaches a treasury, and a legislature decides what happens after. Goods tax works so. Customs works so. Across every one of them a payer surrenders both money and any say over its destination, and that surrender is what makes it a tax.

One levy breaks that. Cross any of three lines — worth above 500 crore, takings above 1,000 crore, or earnings above 5 crore in a year — and Section 135 of India's Companies Act compels a business to lay out near two rupees in each hundred of average earnings across three prior years on public good.1 Spending is compelled. Amount is compelled by formula. Naming each project in a yearly report is compelled. Moving unspent sums out is compelled.

Choosing who receives it is not compelled at all. A board picks, alone, and no public body reviews that pick.

Scale turns that gap from a curiosity into something worth measuring. In FY24, 27,188 businesses laid out ₹34,908.75 crore under this rule.2 A year later that reached ₹40,794 crore.3 Across ten years since a rule began, above ₹1.22 lakh crore has moved.4 A sum near 35,000 crore a year is larger than what several Indian states raise on their own account, and every rupee of it gets aimed by some twenty-seven thousand private boards.

So a question worth asking before any table: if you compel a payment but leave its destination to whoever pays, where would you expect that money to end up — and does filed data show it ending up there?

Three things follow from a payer choosing, and each one is testable against numbers already filed. Money should cluster where payers sit, not where need sits. Money should reach carriers a payer knows or controls, since choosing costs effort and a known carrier costs none. And money should move on a payer's calendar wherever a calendar bends.

Before testing any of that, a warning about arithmetic, because it disables most of what gets written on this subject.

Four different totals exist for FY24, and each is correct. A national portal covering all 27,188 businesses records ₹34,908.75 crore.2 A study of 2,020 businesses examined for district-level aim records a pool of ₹19,208 crore.5 Listed businesses alone laid out ₹17,967 crore against a requirement of ₹18,309 crore.6 And one widely carried report on state shares computes against a base of ₹12,897 crore.7

Watch what that does. Maharashtra received ₹6,066 crore in FY24.7 Against a base of ₹12,897 crore that is 47 in each hundred, and it was published so. Against a national total of ₹34,909 crore it is 17 in each hundred, and that was published too.2 Same state, same year, same rupees, and a share that differs by near three times depending on which denominator a writer chose. Neither figure is wrong. Any sentence of shape "X per cent of CSR" that fails to name its denominator carries no information at all — and most such sentences do fail to name it.

That is not a quibble about care. It is a first demonstration of a pattern this report is about: where a number has several true versions, whoever speaks picks one, and picking runs toward whatever a speaker wants shown. It happens inside company filings. It happens inside writing that criticises company filings, no less. Every figure below therefore carries its base.

Read those last two rows together, since they are a second lesson in denominators. Districts India's own government marks as most in need took 12 in each hundred of one study's pool and 4.5 in each hundred of all inflows.5 That second figure has climbed — it stood at 1.3 in FY15 — so direction is right and pace is slow. Eleven years of a rule moved a share for India's neediest districts by about three points.

Two further measurements sharpen it. Of 2,020 businesses carrying an obligation in FY24, only 397 — near one in five — laid out anything at all in those districts.5 And six states holding above 60 in each hundred of India's most-in-need districts draw under 20 in each hundred of a whole pool.5,8

A separate study puts a same shape a different way, and its wording is worth keeping: only about 30 in each hundred of rural districts receive money aligned to what they need. Across a rest, 23 in each hundred show a complete mismatch between money received and need measured, and 47 in each hundred show partial mismatch.8 Near three-quarters of district-aimed money concentrates in under 200 districts, many of them among India's less poor.

Seven states — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Delhi — draw a large share. Six states — Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Bihar, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh, and a north-east — hold most of what government itself marks as neediest and draw little. Which of those two lists matches where large businesses keep their head offices?

Nobody needs an accusation here, and none is offered. A rule invites spending in a local area, and local is where works stand. Every board following that invitation acts inside law and inside good sense. An aggregate effect is what it is: a levy raised on profit across a whole country, aimed at towns where profit gets booked. Had those same rupees passed through a treasury they would have been aimed by a different politics, better or worse, but not by corporate geography.

Sector concentration runs alongside. Education drew above ₹12,000 crore in FY24 and health above ₹7,000 crore,2 and education, health and rural poverty together take above 60 in each hundred.8 Those are what a large organisation can spend on at scale, report on without dispute, and photograph. Care of land and air, and building of livelihoods, draw less.

Who carries it, and why does nobody know?

Now a second prediction: money should reach carriers a payer knows or controls. Testing it needs a number, so start by naming which number, then look for it.

A carrier must register once with a central registry through a form and receives a number that follows it thereafter, shaped as CSR and eight digits. Registration became compulsory from 1 April 2021, and after that date no trust, society or body may receive this money within law without one.1 By October 2021, 17,130 carriers had registered.9

Every business must name, in a yearly report, each project and each carrier with its registration number. Every business must also publish, on its own account, a list of payments to kin — money reaching people and bodies connected to whoever runs it. Both documents are public. Both are filed to a same registry. So this question is answerable by arithmetic alone:

Out of ₹34,909 crore compelled from Indian businesses in one year, how much reached a body that a paying business, or a household running it, founded itself?

That number is not published anywhere. Not by a ministry, not by a national portal, not by any of several bodies producing yearly reports on this subject, and not by a rating house. Its inputs are filed. A join needed to produce it — carrier name and registration number from one filing, against connected-party lists from another — is mechanical. Nobody publishes a result.

Which leaves indirect evidence, and it points one way. Rules permit a business to route through a body it founded itself, and go further: an outside carrier must show three years of track record, while a carrier a business or its group set up need show none.1 Law made a house vehicle easier to use than a stranger. Across large Indian pharmaceutical businesses, own foundations are close to standard practice — Sun Pharma, Cipla and Divi's each run one.10 And one estimate widely repeated in that field holds that 85 in each hundred of this money reaches a small set of large carriers already known to corporate teams.11

That 85 figure comes from advocacy writing, not from a filed source, and is carried here as a claim by others, not as a measurement. It is stated because a gap it points at is real, and because a reader deserves to know a difference between a number filed and a number asserted. Everything else on this page carries a filed base.

Which leaves aggregates unable to finish this. Aggregates show shape. They cannot show mechanism, because mechanism sits in individual ledgers. So take one mid-sized listed business and read its own filings across six years: ASK Automotive Limited, which makes brake parts for two-wheelers and carries an unqualified audit opinion.

Whose hands those are needs no inference. A filing lodged with an exchange calls that trust "born out of chairman & MD Kuldip Singh Rathee and his wife." A list of payments to kin files AHSAAS under bodies "in which directors of the Company and their relatives are able to exercise significant influence." Both published. Neither buried. They sit in separate documents, and few readers hold both at once.

Now something an aggregate could never surface. Look at that row again, because its shape is impossible.

An obligation runs at two in each hundred of a three-year average of earnings. A three-year average moves in gentle steps by construction. So how does a payment against it fall from 3.89 to 0.71, then climb above five times back to 3.72?

It cannot, if that row records an obligation. Arithmetic forbids it: a rolling three-year mean of a rising profit series cannot fall 82 in each hundred in one year and then rise 5.2 times across two.

So that row records something else, and naming what resolves it. A row records payments into a carrier, not spending by a carrier. Those are separate events, and a gap between them is where a house vehicle parts company with a stranger. Pay an unrelated body and you pay against milestones it must evidence, since it answers to its own board and has no reason to hold your cash. Pay a body your own household founded and timing becomes yours: money may sit inside it, across years, and a smooth obligation can be met by lumpy transfers into a pot you already control.

Nothing above shows a rupee gone astray, and nothing here asserts one. Both readings remain live — a rule change in 2021 requiring unspent sums to move into a ring-fenced account could produce bunching of this kind, and so could multi-year projects. What separates them is a document: an annexure splitting amount spent from amount transferred, and a trust's own filed accounts. Until one of those gets pulled, a shape stands as a question a board can answer in a sentence and has not been asked.

An aggregate says a same thing about a whole country. Money moving into ring-fenced accounts rose from ₹1,643 crore in FY23 to ₹2,329 crore in FY24, a climb near 42 in each hundred.6,12 Across listed businesses, a gap between what was required and what was laid out widened from ₹189 crore (required 15,713, laid out 15,524) to ₹342 crore (required 18,309, laid out 17,967).6 More money is being parked, and a shortfall is growing, in a period when total spending climbed at speed.

One tool falls out of all this, and it works on any business you care about. Since an amount is fixed by formula at two in each hundred of a three-year mean, that formula inverts. Multiply a business's obligation by fifty and you recover its implied three-year average earnings — a figure computed under Section 198 in place of reported profit, and on a standalone not a group basis, so treat it as a cross-check and not a substitute. Its value is that it is formula-bound: it is one of few published figures a board cannot shade by picking a basis, because a basis is written into law. Where an inverted figure and a reported figure disagree across several years, one of them needs explaining.

So turn all of it into work a reader can do. Every test below runs on documents anyone can pull, without paid access and without a source inside.

Ten questions. Nine of them answer from one document, and a tenth needs a registry search taking minutes. No analyst house publishes answers to these across even a hundred companies, though inputs for all 27,188 sit filed with one ministry.

Which is a last observation, and a largest one. A gap this report describes is not a gap in law and not a gap in disclosure. Law compels naming every carrier. Law compels flagging every connected body. Businesses comply. A gap sits between two filings that nobody joins — and it persists because joining them is dull, because no house gets paid to do it, and because a result would embarrass customers of whoever did.

None of that requires a conspiracy. It requires only that a tedious join stay undone, which is a most reliable thing in any field. That join costs an afternoon for one company. For all 27,188, it costs a database and a fortnight, and it would settle a question this page could only ask.

On what this report is built from. Aggregate figures come from secondary reporting of primary sources — India's National CSR Portal, a CRISIL study, Prime Database, and a Development Intelligence Unit report — and are cited below to whichever account was read. They have not been rebuilt from ministry filings, and are carried at a confidence of published aggregates, not of primary documents. Company-level figures for ASK Automotive come from its own filings and are stated at full confidence: yearly reports FY21 through FY26, audited group accounts signed without objection by Walker Chandiok & Co LLP, and a filing lodged with BSE. Passages inside quotation marks stand word for word as filed. Sums add to ₹15.08 crore across six years. Descriptions of what law requires are a plain reading of Section 135 of India's Companies Act 2013 and rules under it including changes of 2021; thresholds and ceilings get revised, so check current text before relying on any of them. Research, not advice on where to put money. No target prices, no recommendations, no holding in anything named.

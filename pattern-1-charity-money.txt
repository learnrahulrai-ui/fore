WHEN LAW COMPELS A GIFT, WHO CHOOSES WHERE IT LANDS?

A pattern worth checking in any Indian business you hold pieces of.
Worked through one company's own filings, FY21 to FY26:
ASK Automotive Limited (NSE: ASKAUTOLTD).

August 2026.

================================================================


START WITH WHAT COMPULSION COVERS, AND WHAT IT LEAVES ALONE

A lord in a bad year could be made to open a granary. Compulsion ran
to opening it. No writ named which village ate, or whose steward
carried grain, or who stood in a square handing out sacks and got
thanked for it by name.

India runs a version of that rule on businesses. Any business past a
certain size must spend a share of what it earns -- near two rupees in
each hundred of average earnings across three prior years -- on public
good. Schools, water, training, sport, care of sick people. Spending is
no choice at all. A board skipping it must explain itself in writing
and carry that explanation into a yearly report where anyone can read
it.

So set down what compulsion reaches, and what it does not:

    Compelled  --  that money leaves a business.
    Compelled  --  that an amount matches a formula.
    Compelled  --  that a report names each project funded.
    Open       --  whose hands carry that money.

That last line is a whole pattern on its own.

Money must go. Nobody is told where. A board picks who carries it, and
picks afresh each year, and no rule says a carrier must be a stranger.
Law permits a business to set up its own trust and route money through
that. A carrier registers once with a national registry and receives a
number that follows it thereafter. Those numbers run in a fixed shape:
CSR followed by eight digits.

Which hands a reader one clean test, needing no training and no
guesswork.

    Open a yearly report. Find a section listing money given away.
    Count how many distinct registry numbers appear across every
    project in it.

A business spreading money across a dozen bodies chosen on merit shows
a dozen numbers. A business handing all of it to one pair of hands
shows one.

SO WHAT DOES ONE COMPANY'S REPORT SHOW?

Money ASK Automotive gave away, by year, in crore of rupees, as filed
in its own accounts:

    FY21      1.02
    FY22      3.89
    FY23      0.71
    FY24      1.29
    FY25      3.72
    FY26      4.45
             -----
    Six yrs  15.08

Projects those sums paid for, as its yearly report lists them:

    Training for work            Jhajjar
    Schooling                    Gurgaon, Rewa, Ghaziabad,
                                 Prayagraj, Delhi
    Village sport                Faridabad, Sonipat, Bhiwani
    Care of land and air         Gurgaon

Nine towns. Four kinds of work. Six years.

Now run that test. Count registry numbers across all of it.

    One.

    CSR00000244 -- AHSAAS.

Not most of that money. Not a large share of it. Every rupee, across
four kinds of work and nine towns, passed through one pair of hands.

Two separate documents carry those two halves, and a reader should
know which carries which. A section on money given away holds that
single registry number. A list of payments to kin, running six years,
holds a line named for AHSAAS with a sum against each year -- those
six sums above, adding to 15.08 crore. Neither document says what a
pair of them says together.


================================================================


AND WHOSE HANDS ARE THOSE?

This part needs no inference, no digging through a registry, no
guessing from surnames. Two documents ASK filed itself answer it, and
they answer it in plain words.

First, a filing lodged with an exchange describes that trust as:

    "born out of chairman & MD Kuldip Singh Rathee and his wife"

Second, a list of payments to kin -- a schedule every listed business
must publish, naming money that went to people connected to whoever
runs it -- carries AHSAAS inside it, under a heading covering bodies:

    "in which directors of the Company and their relatives are able
     to exercise significant influence"

Read those two together and a shape settles.

A duty owed by a business to strangers gets discharged through a body
a chairman and his wife brought into being, which that business's own
accountants classify as connected to whoever runs it. Both facts are
published. Neither is buried. They sit in separate documents that few
readers ever hold at one time.


================================================================


WHAT DOES AND DOES NOT FOLLOW FROM THAT

Being strict here matters more than scoring a point, so take both
sides.

What cannot be said:

    Nothing above shows a rupee went astray. Those schools may be
    real schools. That training may train. A trust founded by a
    family may run better than a stranger would, since a founder
    watches what carries his name. Law permits every step. An
    auditor signed without objection. Many businesses do this, and
    a family foundation is an ordinary vehicle across much of a
    trading world.

What does follow:

    A compelled outflow becomes a free choice about destination, and
    that choice went one way, six years running, without exception
    and without any sign of a contest.

    Money that has left a business is gone to whoever holds pieces of
    it, whichever body carries it. What a household gains is a
    different thing from cash: a standing corpus it decides on, a
    name attached to nine towns of good work, seats at a table, and
    thanks in a square. None of that shows in any account.

    A sum tracks earnings by formula, so it swells as a business
    swells. FY26 at 4.45 crore is largest yet. It is an income that
    grows on its own, needing no fresh decision.

    Nobody outside can check whether merit chose that carrier,
    because no competing carrier was ever named.

HOW TO RUN THIS ON ANY BUSINESS IN TEN MINUTES

Nothing below needs paid access, training, or a source inside.

    1.  Pull a yearly report. Find a section on money given away.
        It sits near a back, among annexures.

    2.  Count distinct registry numbers, shape CSR00000000. One
        number across many projects is a finding. A dozen numbers
        is not.

    3.  Take whatever name carries that number, and search a list of
        payments to kin in that same report. If a name appears there,
        a business has told you itself that a carrier is connected.

    4.  Search a whole report for that carrier's name. A filing
        somewhere often states who founded it, in words as plain as
        those quoted above.

    5.  Set sums by year beside earnings by year. A ratio holding
        near two in a hundred says a business spends what law asks
        and no more, and that a carrier holds an income which climbs
        as a business climbs.

If steps two, three and four all land on one name, you have this
pattern. What you do with it is a separate question, and this page
takes no view on that.
